How to use this page

This is a jumping-off point for Assessment Task #1, not a model answer. It shows how to plan the task, how to research an incident, and how to set out a DFD, a structure chart, a flowchart and a data dictionary in NESA notation. The worked example is a starting point. Your analysis, design choices and evaluation must be your own.

Before you start, ask Which part of my analysis will a reader have to take on trust, and what evidence would convince them?

Start here The task at a glance

The task at a glance

🎯 (SE-12-03, SE-12-04, SE-12-05, SE-12-07)

You will analyse one real, documented security breach. You will trace its failures, explain how emerging technology and legacy systems shaped it, and design a layered, secure redesign. You will then evaluate the privacy, resilience, ethical and legal implications. You act as the Security Architect.

Two formats, one standard. You can submit a technical report (PDF or DOCX) or an interactive website. Both must cover all six sections. Both must use NESA notation for every diagram. Your teacher's brief sets out the exact requirements.

How you will be marked

Each section is marked against two criteria. Knowledge is what you understand and can explain. Application is what you do with that understanding in your analysis, design and evaluation of your incident. Each criterion is placed in one of six bands, and each band shows its mark range. The band descriptions use the NESA performance band language for Software Engineering. A criterion with no evidence in your work scores 0.

KnowledgeWhat you understand and can explain, such as the causes of an incident or how a security control works.
ApplicationWhat you do with that understanding: your diagrams, design, code, testing and evaluation.
CommunicationHow logically and clearly you present your work, using correct terms, conventions and methods. It is judged across your whole submission.

The evidence rule

Your incident must be documented in at least three reliable sources. At least one must be primary: an official investigation, a regulator's finding, a vendor advisory or a CVE record. Secondary news reports are useful for context, but they can't carry the whole analysis on their own. Hypothetical examples are not accepted.

Source typeExamplesUse it for
PrimaryGovernment alerts, regulator findings, post-incident reviews, vendor advisories, CVE recordsFacts, root cause, remediation
SecondaryNews reports and specialist security journalismContext, timelines, reactions
AvoidBlogs with no sources, AI-generated summaries, sites you can't dateNothing that the analysis depends on
Check dates and version numbers. Many reports change after release, and some incidents are still being investigated. Record the date you read each source, and note any facts that are still disputed.
Worked starter Conti ransomware and the HSE (2021)

Worked starter: the Conti attack on the HSE

The Health Service Executive (HSE) is Ireland's public health service. In May 2021 it was hit by a human-operated Conti ransomware attack. This section sets out what the official sources say, and gives you starter diagrams in NESA notation. Extend them with your own research. Do not copy them.

March to May 2021The attackers move through the network and reach Active Directory. The review, written by PwC, says the attackers probably exploited an unpatched known vulnerability. The full report does not identify it.The Register (10 December 2021)

What the sources don't tell you: the sources reviewed here don't say whether a ransom was paid, whether data was leaked, what the attack cost, or how many systems were affected in total. Find out from the primary sources before you claim any of these, and say clearly when a fact is unknown.

  • Find the vulnerability that allowed the move into Active Directory, if a source identifies it.
  • Redesign the failures. The detection gap, the unpatched vulnerability and the flat network are your starting points for Section 2.
  • Add your own analysis of the emerging-technology and legacy-system factors in Section 1, and make it specific to this incident.

Exam-style practice: sample answers from identify to explain

These four items use the Conti attack as their stimulus, and move from the least demanding verb to the most demanding. They are written for this page, not taken from an HSC paper. The NESA glossary does not define these command terms, so the meanings used here are the usual HSC meanings: identify names, outline gives the main steps in order, describe gives features and what they do, and explain gives causes and their effects. Attempt each item before you read its sample answer.

Item 1: Identify (2 marks)

Identify two actions that the HSE or the NCSC took or recommended to contain the attack.

Sample answer: (1) The HSE shut down all of its IT systems as a precaution on 14 May 2021. (2) The NCSC recommended isolating the domain controllers.

Marker's note: two named, specific actions are enough. "Responded to the attack" or "told people about it" does not earn a mark, because it is not a containment action.

Item 2: Outline (3 marks)

Outline the sequence of events from the phishing email to the Conti payload running.

Sample answer:

  1. On 16 March 2021 a phishing email carrying a malicious Excel file reached the HSE, and a user opened it on the Patient Zero workstation.
  2. The attackers deployed tools such as Cobalt Strike and Mimikatz. Antivirus detected them on Patient Zero, but Hospital C did not start an incident response investigation.
  3. The attackers moved through the network to Active Directory, probably using an unpatched known vulnerability.
  4. On 14 May 2021 the Conti payload ran, launched with psexec.exe using compromised user credentials.

Marker's note: the order is part of the answer. Each step needs its date or its effect, and the steps should be the main ones only.

Item 3: Describe (3 marks)

Describe how the attackers used compromised credentials to move the ransomware onto other computers.

Sample answer: The attackers used psexec.exe, a remote-execution tool, to run the Conti v3 executable on endpoints where they held compromised user credentials. Because the credentials were valid, the payload could start on machines the attackers had not themselves broken into. The NCSC's analysis suggests that Cobalt Strike beacons on systems point to its use for lateral movement before the payload ran.

Marker's note: a describe answer gives the feature (psexec, valid credentials) and what it achieved (the payload started on other endpoints). A list of tool names with no link to the attack scores low.

Item 4: Explain (5 marks)

Explain why the attack was able to spread across the HSE network before it was contained.

Sample answer: The attack spread because the attackers had time and room to move, and the warning signs were not acted on. Antivirus detected Cobalt Strike and Mimikatz on Patient Zero, but when Hospital C asked its external provider about the alerts on 10 May, the risk was judged low and no investigation began. This gave the attackers time to reach Active Directory, which PwC's review says they probably did by exploiting an unpatched known vulnerability. Once there, they could run the payload with compromised credentials. The effect was severe: the HSE shut down all its IT systems as a precaution on 14 May, and non-emergency procedures were postponed while hospitals used their business continuity plans.

Marker's note: a full answer links each cause to an effect, and uses the scenario's own evidence. The "why" must be explained, not just stated: "the attackers were skilled" is a claim, but "the alert was dismissed, so nothing stopped the next step" is an explanation.

Exam tip: match the depth to the verb A one-word verb such as identify needs precise facts and no reasoning. Outline needs the steps in order, with a short point for each. Describe needs the feature and what it achieved. Explain needs a cause, the link between cause and effect, and evidence. Writing a long explanation for an identify item wastes time, and writing a list for an explain item loses most of the marks.

Diagram checklist

  • DFD: circles for processes, open-ended rectangles or two parallel lines for data stores, rectangles for external entities, labelled curved arrows for flows.
  • Structure chart: open circles for data, filled circles for flags, a small diamond for an optional call, curved arrows for repetition.
  • Flowchart: BEGIN and END terminators, process boxes, decision diamonds with labelled branches, and I/O and subprogram symbols where needed.
  • Class diagram: classes with attributes and methods, and labelled relationships with multiplicity.
  • Check each diagram against the NESA Software Engineering Course Specifications (updated February 2025) before you submit it.

Secure and insecure code side by side

Section 2 of the task asks for code that prevents the Section 1 vulnerability. This example shows one common case: SQL injection through a username lookup. Each version is shown in Python, with NESA pseudocode alongside. The pseudocode follows the Course Specifications: capitalised keywords, paired structures such as IF and ENDIF, and indentation.

The insecure version

The input is pasted straight into the SQL text, so the input can change what the query does.

Python: insecure
import sqlite3

def find_user(conn, username):
    query = "SELECT id, username FROM users WHERE username = '" + username + "'"
    return conn.execute(query).fetchall()

conn = sqlite3.connect(":memory:")
conn.execute("CREATE TABLE users (id INTEGER, username TEXT)")
conn.executemany("INSERT INTO users VALUES (?, ?)", [(1, "alice"), (2, "bob_smith")])

print("Normal input:", find_user(conn, "alice"))
print("Attack input:", find_user(conn, "' OR '1'='1"))
Expected output
Normal input: [(1, 'alice')]
Attack input: [(1, 'alice'), (2, 'bob_smith')]

The secure version

The input is checked first, and the query uses a placeholder. The database then treats the value as data, never as SQL.

Python: secure
import re
import sqlite3

USERNAME_PATTERN = re.compile(r"^[A-Za-z0-9_]{3,20}$")

def find_user(conn, username):
    if not USERNAME_PATTERN.fullmatch(username):
        raise ValueError("Invalid username")
    query = "SELECT id, username FROM users WHERE username = ?"
    return conn.execute(query, (username,)).fetchall()

conn = sqlite3.connect(":memory:")
conn.execute("CREATE TABLE users (id INTEGER, username TEXT)")
conn.executemany("INSERT INTO users VALUES (?, ?)", [(1, "alice"), (2, "bob_smith")])

print("Normal input:", find_user(conn, "alice"))
try:
    find_user(conn, "' OR '1'='1")
except ValueError as error:
    print("Attack input rejected:", error)
Expected output
Normal input: [(1, 'alice')]
Attack input rejected: Invalid username

The same logic in NESA pseudocode

Insecure:

NESA pseudocode: insecure
BEGIN FindUser (username)
    query = "SELECT id, username FROM users WHERE username = '" + username + "'"
    RETURN the rows that query finds
END FindUser (username)

Secure:

NESA pseudocode: secure
BEGIN FindUser (username)
    IF username is not 3 to 20 letters, digits or underscores THEN
        Raise an error: "Invalid username"
    ENDIF
    query = "SELECT id, username FROM users WHERE username = ?"
    RETURN the rows that query finds, using username as the parameter
END FindUser (username)

What to notice

  • Validation rejects any input that is not a valid username, before it reaches the database.
  • The parameterised query uses ? as a placeholder. The value is passed separately, so it is never read as SQL.
  • Error handling returns one short message. It does not show the query or the database's own error.

Test both versions

TestInputInsecure resultSecure result
Normal usernamealiceReturns aliceReturns alice
SQL injection' OR '1'='1Returns every userRejected: Invalid username
In your report. Show the insecure code and the attack input with its result. Then show the secure code, and name the line that stops the attack. Name each control: validation, parameterised query and error handling.

Current event: a PHP vulnerability (October 2026)

This is a teacher-led example, not a required scenario. It shows how a live vulnerability is reported, and how facts can be confirmed or left open. Information is current as at 22 October 2026, and it may change.

Confirmed by the advisory: CERT-EU Security Advisory 2026-018, published 21 October 2026, reports two critical flaws in PHP. Both are rated CVSS 9.2. CVE-2026-109618 allows unauthenticated remote code execution through memory corruption during out-of-memory handling. CVE-2026-109619 allows unauthenticated remote code execution through stale global state, where internal request state can survive after its memory has been freed. The advisory also says that only an update fully fixes these vulnerabilities; settings reduce exposure but do not make an installation safe. Fixed versions are PHP 8.5.12, 8.4.27, 8.3.36 and 8.2.35. PHP 8.1 and earlier are end-of-life, with minimal backports available for some versions.
Not yet confirmed: the advisory says exploitation is not confirmed. It says public technical details and proofs of concept are circulating, which "raises the likelihood that attackers are already exploiting the vulnerabilities". Some media reporting uses the name "PHP2Shell". That name does not appear in the CERT-EU advisory, and this review could not find a primary source for it or for claims of active exploitation. Do not cite either without a primary source. Report the difference between confirmed and reported facts.

Discussion questions

  1. Which facts in the advisory are confirmed, and which are only likely?
  2. Why does the advisory say that "only an update fully fixes these vulnerabilities", and what does that say about relying on temporary settings?
  3. Which kinds of software are affected, and how would a developer find out whether their application relies on PHP?
  4. Which parts of the secure architecture in this notes site would reduce the harm from a remote code execution flaw?

Using AI tools

Your teacher's brief sets the rules. Read the Responsible AI Use Policy in the brief before you start.

  • Allowed, if disclosed: brainstorming, explaining a concept, checking grammar, summarising a long source, suggesting a diagram structure, and syntax help for small utility code.
  • Diagrams: AI may suggest a structure for a diagram. You must draw the final diagram yourself in NESA notation, and check it against the Course Specifications.
  • Code: AI may help with utility functions and syntax. Your defensive design and your test cases must be your own.
  • Not allowed: writing your analysis, your design or your evaluation; generating the core code or pseudocode; or copying AI text into your report without changing and checking it.

Record every substantial use in the AI Assistance Disclosure Log, placed before your reference list.

Submission checklist

  • Submitted through the LMS, by the due date in your brief. Email submissions are not accepted.
  • All six sections are present, each with the deliverables listed for it.
  • Every diagram follows NESA notation, and you have checked each one against the Course Specifications.
  • Every claim about the incident is backed by a cited source. You have at least three sources, and at least one is primary.
  • Every control in Section 2 links back to a vulnerability from Section 1.
  • Your testing protocol includes at least one test case with its expected result.
  • Your process diary has at least three dated entries.
  • Your AI Assistance Disclosure Log is complete, and sits before your references.
  • Website only: a direct link to the public site, plus a PDF backup of the full site (all six sections).
  • Website only: a timestamped screenshot or video taken immediately before the due date.
