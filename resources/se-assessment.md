## 1. Course structure

### 1.1 Course pattern and indicative hours

Verified against [SYL-OVERVIEW] ("Course structure and requirements").

| Year | Focus area | Indicative hours |
|---|---|---|
| Year 11 (120 hours) | Programming Fundamentals | 40 |
| Year 11 | The Object-Oriented Paradigm | 40 |
| Year 11 | Programming Mechatronics | 40 |
| Year 12 (120 hours) | Secure Software Architecture | 30 |
| Year 12 | Programming for the Web | 30 |
| Year 12 | Software Automation | 30 |
| Year 12 | Software Engineering Project | 30 |

"Indicative hours" is NESA's wording; the hours are indicative, not mandatory.

Course details [SYL-OVERVIEW]: two 2-unit courses, Software Engineering Year 11 (course code 11345) and Year 12 (15365). Prerequisites: Year 11 – nil; Year 12 – Software Engineering (Year 11, 2 units): 11345. Co-requisites: nil. Exclusions (both years): Computing Technology Life Skills (16623) and Technology Life Skills (16686).

### 1.2 Organisation and course description (NESA wording)

[SYL-OVERVIEW], "Organisation of Software Engineering 11–12":

> "The Year 11 course provides students with opportunities to develop and apply an understanding of the fundamental elements involved in creating software."
>
> "The Year 12 course provides students with opportunities to extend their knowledge, understanding and skills in the development of software. A major software engineering project provides students with the opportunity to further develop project management skills."

Course description [SYL-OVERVIEW]: "Software Engineering 11–12 provides students the opportunity to develop knowledge, understanding and skills in software engineering, hardware and software integration, and in the development, implementation and evaluation of computer programs. "What students learn": students learn to

### 1.3 Course Specifications are part of the course

[SYL-OVERVIEW]: "The Software Engineering Course Specifications are an integral part of the course content for Year 11 and Year 12 and indicate the depth of study required for some concepts in the Software Engineering 11–12 Syllabus. The current [SPECS] PDF is dated **March 2026** (33 pages). Python: [SPECS] p. 32: "Students are expected to be able to code using the Python programming language." ## 2. Project requirements

### 2.1 What the syllabus documents say

- **Year 11:** practical experiences and project work are part of every Year 11 focus area. NESA's teaching advice gives "Project work advice" for each Year 11 focus area [TA, Year 11]:
  - Programming Fundamentals: students "apply core knowledge and skills to develop project(s) that incorporate standard algorithmic structures converted into programming code. Projects could include the conversion of a standard board game into a computer game, designing a memory game, maze or arcade-style video game."
  - The Object-Oriented Paradigm: "Projects could include a quiz-based application, stopwatch and alarm clock, a bulk file renaming tool, a simple graphic user interface (GUI) or application programming interface (API)."
  - Programming Mechatronics: students "develop project(s) by building and programming a mechatronic system using a range of sensors, actuators and end effectors. Projects explore the use and application of robotics, controllers and the programming of these devices using a text-based, OOP language."
- **Year 12 Software Engineering Project (30 indicative hours)** [TA, Year 12]: "The Software Engineering Project is to be completed in Year 12 and will require students to develop a project of their choice based on the knowledge and skills developed in Year 11 and Year 12. Students have an opportunity to develop deeper knowledge, understanding and skills in an area of interest as they engage in a software engineering project that addresses an identified real-world problem. Students refine and apply project-management skills as they develop, document, implement and evaluate their project solution. Learning in this focus area emphasises project-management and software development approaches, explores the tools used by software designers and engineers, and explores the concept of delivering solutions for a client. Highly valued industry skills such as collaboration and communication are developed through project work where students consider involving and empowering clients, responding to stakeholders, and negotiating with peers. Teachers are encouraged to integrate the learning content of this focus area within all other Year 12 focus areas."
- The Software Automation teaching advice adds: "This highlights opportunities for students to incorporate software automation into their Software Engineering project." [TA, Year 12]
- **Syllabus content for the project** [SYL-CONTENT, Year 12, "Software engineering project"; outcomes SE-12-01 to SE-12-09] is organised in four subheadings: Identifying and defining; Research and planning; Producing and implementing; Testing and evaluating. Notable dot points (verbatim): "Investigate types of software implementation methods" (direct, phased, parallel, pilot); "Research and use the Waterfall software development approach"; "Research and use the Agile software development approach"; "Research the WAgile software development approach"; "Apply project management to plan and conduct the development and implementation of a project and software engineering solution" (scheduling and tracking using a software tool, including Gantt charts; using collaboration tools); "Implement version control when developing a software engineering solution"; "Demonstrate the use of programmed data backup"; "Present a software engineering solution using presentation software"; "Propose an additional innovative solution using a prototype and user interface (UI) design"; "Use a language-dependent code optimisation technique"; "Evaluate the effectiveness of a software engineering solution" (test plan, path and boundary testing, comparing actual and expected output). A verbatim copy of the full content is in `nesa-syllabus-content.md`.
- **Project documentation notations** are specified in [SPECS]: Gantt charts and process diaries / log books (pp. 11–12), data flow diagrams, structure charts, data dictionary, class diagrams, storyboard and decision trees (pp. 5–10).
- **Sample time allocation (illustrative only):** NESA's sample unit "Software Engineering Project" states "Duration: 30 hours over 3 terms" and "The Software Engineering Project is developed in the classroom under the supervision of the teacher"; sample scope and sequence B shows the project unit "delivered concurrently with other focus areas (30 hours over 3 terms)" and states "The final assessment task is embedded in this unit." Sample unit: https://library.curriculum.nsw.edu.au/341419dc-8ec2-0289-7225-6db7f2d751ef/e265aee2-4be3-4e2b-94cf-c40830a6da90/software-engineering-11-12-2022-sample-unit-year-12-software-engineering-project.docx ; scope and sequence B: https://library.curriculum.nsw.edu.au/341419dc-8ec2-0289-7225-6db7f2d751ef/eed22c34-4fe8-429d-86d4-71e03a985fe5/software-engineering-11-12-2022-sample-scope-and-sequence-b-year-12-120-hours.DOCX . These are samples "provided by NESA to illustrate teaching, learning and assessment"; they are not requirements.

### 2.2 What is NOT specified (checked; not found)

- NESA does **not** publish, on [SBA], [HSC-EXAM], [TA], [SYL-CONTENT] or [SYL-OVERVIEW], a mandatory project task, project weighting, minimum or maximum project length, word limit, submission requirement, or required number of project tasks for Software Engineering. The project is a focus area with 30 indicative hours; whether and how it is assessed in the school-based program is a school decision within the rules in section 3.
- **There is no HSC practical examination or submitted major work.** [HSC-EXAM] describes a single online written examination only (section 4); the 2025 exam pack contains only an online exam, marking guidelines, marker feedback and sample responses [EXAM-2025]. The syllabus overview does use the phrase "A major software engineering project" [SYL-OVERVIEW], but NESA's take-home exemption list (section 3.3) does not include Software Engineering.

## 3. School-based assessment requirements

The Year 11 and Year 12 requirements below are word-for-word the same in structure for Software Engineering and Enterprise Computing.

### 3.1 Year 11 (Preliminary) [SBA, "Year 11 Software Engineering school-based assessment"]

- Grades: "Schools are required to submit to NESA a grade for each student based on their achievement at the end of the course. Teachers use professional, on-balance judgement to allocate grades based on the Common Grade Scale for Preliminary courses."
- **Mandatory components and weightings:** "The course components and component weightings for Year 11 are mandatory."

| Course component | Weighting % |
|---|---|
| Knowledge and understanding of course content | 50 |
| Knowledge and skills in the practical application of the content | 50 |

- Number, type and weighting of tasks: "Schools have authority to determine the number, type and weighting of assessment tasks, subject to a limit of one take-home task worth no more than 15%. See ACE 2.1 for more information." NESA therefore does **not** fix a number of tasks for Year 11.
- Guidance (not mandatory): "When developing assessment programs, schools should: use a variety of assessment tasks across the year to give students opportunities to demonstrate their knowledge, skills and understanding in different ways; give individual assessment tasks a weighting between 10% and 40%."

### 3.2 Year 12 (HSC school-based assessment) [SBA, "Year 12 Software Engineering school-based assessment"]

- "NESA requires schools to submit a school-based assessment mark for each Year 12 student who completes this course. Formal school-based assessment tasks should reflect the syllabus outcomes and content. The mark submitted by the school provides a summation of each student's achievement measured at several points throughout the course."
- **Mandatory components and weightings:** "The course components and component weightings for Year 12 are mandatory."

| Course component | Weighting % |
|---|---|
| Knowledge and understanding of course content | 50 |
| Knowledge and skills in the practical application of the content | 50 |

- Number, type and weighting of tasks: "Schools have authority to determine the number, type and weighting of assessment tasks, subject to a limit of one take-home task worth no more than 15%." No number of tasks is fixed. The same two guidance bullets apply (variety of tasks; individual task weighting between 10% and 40%).
- The split between the school-based mark and the HSC examination mark is **not stated** on the Software Engineering pages checked ([SBA], [HSC-EXAM], [STANDARDS], [ACE] 2.1). It is not asserted here.
- Sample assessment schedules and task notifications: NESA lists headings "Sample assessment schedules" and "Sample assessment task notifications" on [SBA], but **no files are attached to either heading** as at 29 September 2026. The page carries this notice: "Sample assessment schedules and tasks are being updated to align with new ACE Rules. More information: New limit on take-home assessment tasks from Term 4 2026." No sample assessment schedule with task numbers or weightings exists for Software Engineering on NESA sites.

### 3.3 The take-home task limit (new in Term 4 2026)

- [SBA] (both years): "subject to a limit of one take-home task worth no more than 15%."
- [NOTICE-29] (NESA 29/26, 31 August 2026, "Effective: Term 4, 2026"): "For Preliminary and HSC courses, school-based assessment programs can have no more than one take-home assessment task, worth a maximum weighting of 15%. Take-home assessments are tasks completed outside a classroom, formal examination setting or teacher-approved monitoring conditions. Schools still decide the number, type and weighting of all in-school assessment tasks."
- Start dates [NOTICE-29]: "For HSC courses, the limit starts from Term 4, 2026. For Preliminary courses, the limit starts from Term 1, 2027."
- Exemption [NOTICE-29]: "The limit does not apply to courses with a major project or an HSC practical exam. In these courses, all assessment tasks in the assessment program are not subject to the limit." The notice's list of exempt courses is exactly: Aboriginal Studies, Society and Culture, Design and Technology, Industrial Technology, Textiles and Design, Visual Arts, Dance, Drama, Music 1, Music 2, English Extension 2, Science Extension, History Extension, Music Extension. It ends: "The limit applies to all other Preliminary and HSC courses."
- **Software Engineering is not on the exempt list, so the limit applies to it in both years.** (This is a reading of NESA's published list, not a sentence NESA writes about Software Engineering specifically. It is consistent with the course having no HSC practical exam or submitted work, section 4, and with the [SBA] pages, which state the limit for Software Engineering directly.)
- ACE rules [ACE]:
  - HSC (2.1.2): "For each HSC course, schools must not exceed one take-home assessment task, worth a maximum of 15%, except for courses with major projects or HSC practical exams that are exempt."
  - Preliminary (2.1.1): "For each Preliminary course, schools must not exceed one take-home assessment task, worth a maximum of 15%, except for courses that are a pre-requisite for a related HSC course with a major project or HSC practical exam that are exempt." (Software Engineering Year 11 is a prerequisite for Software Engineering Year 12, which is not exempt, so the Preliminary exemption does not apply.)
  - [ACE] glossary: "Take-home assessment tasks are assessments completed that are not in-school assessment tasks."
  - [ACE]: "Schools have the authority to determine the number, type and weighting of in-school assessment tasks."
- [NOTICE-34] (NESA 34/26, 16 September 2026, "Effective: Immediately"): changes to ACE rules 2.1.1 (Preliminary course school-based assessment), 2.1.2 (HSC course school-based assessment), 2.2.2 (Submitting HSC school-based assessment marks to NESA), 2.5.2 (Certifying and submitting HSC projects, submitted works and performances) and 10.1.2 (Developing a school malpractice policy). Changes apply to "Year 12 students commencing HSC courses from Term 4, 2026; Year 11 students commencing Preliminary courses from Term 1, 2027; Year 11 students studying under a compressed curriculum model and commencing their Preliminary courses from Week 6, Term 4 of 2026." NESA points to the "ACE Record of Changes" for a summary of each change (not read here).
- What [NOTICE-29] tells schools to do now: "Review your 2027 HSC and Preliminary assessment schedules. In each course, ensure you have no more than one take-home assessment task worth a maximum weighting of 15%. Check your malpractice policy does not rely on the use of AI tools to detect malpractice."

### 3.4 Generative AI (ACE rule) [ACE], [NOTICE-29]

- [ACE]: "Schools must design formal assessment tasks to ensure marks are reflective of a student's own knowledge, skills and understanding, and not the use of generative artificial intelligence."
- [ACE] (teachers): "award marks based on evidence of the student's own knowledge, skills and understanding, and not the use of generative artificial intelligence"; principals certify submitted marks on the same basis.
- ACE 10.1.2 "Developing a school malpractice policy" (https://curriculum.nsw.edu.au/ace-rules/ace10/malpractice): schools must "not rely on software tools for the detection of generative artificial intelligence when determining whether malpractice has occured [sic]."
- [ACE]: "Schools and school sectors remain responsible for policies concerning the use of AI in schools, including whether generative AI is permissible for a school-based assessment task or course."
- [NOTICE-29]: "Marks must not reflect a student's use of generative AI." "Schools should not rely on AI detection tools as the main safeguard against malpractice. If used at all, detection software should be treated as only one input for teacher professional review, not as sole proof of AI use or malpractice."

### 3.5 Other assessment-program rules [ACE]

- Written notice: "Schools must provide students with sufficient written notice, typically 14 calendar days, of an upcoming assessment task." The notification must include the weighting, syllabus outcomes assessed, type of task, scheduled date and time, and marking criteria "(where appropriate)".
- At the start of each course the school must provide the formal assessment program, "with assessment schedules that detail: syllabus outcomes assessed in each assessment task, and the course components and component weightings allocated to each assessment task, and when each assessment task is to be attempted or submitted."
- Schools must give written advice on policies for malpractice, absence, late submission, illness/misadventure, invalid or unreliable results and appeals.
- Earlier changes recorded in [ROC]: "Year 11 school-based assessment requirements were amended to provide greater flexibility to schools" (December 2023) and "The information for Year 12 school-based assessment has changed. Refer to Official Notice NESA 32/24" (August 2024).

## 4. HSC examination specifications

Verbatim from [HSC-EXAM] ("Software Engineering HSC examination specifications"):

- "The examination will be worth 80 marks."
- "It will be undertaken by students using a computer."
- "The time allowed is 2 hours and 30 minutes including 10 minutes reading time."
- "Questions may contain stimulus material."
- "Headphones will be required for questions with video and audio stimulus."
- "The examination will consist of two types of test item."

| Item type | Approximate marks | Details (verbatim) |
|---|---|---|
| **Objective-response items** | approx. 20 marks | "Objective-response items may share stimulus with other objective-response items or short-answer items." "Each item will be worth 1 to 4 marks." "There will be 14 to 18 items in total." |
| **Short-answer items** | approx. 60 marks | "Short-answer items may share stimulus with other short-answer items or objective-response items." "At least three items will be worth 4 to 8 marks." "There will be 16 to 18 items in total." |

Format summary: **online** (computer-based) exam; no downloadable paper. The specification describes **two item types, not "Section I / Section II"**; it does not describe the paper as sectioned. NESA's page also states:

- "The external HSC examination measures student achievement in a range of syllabus outcomes." The examination and its marking relate to the syllabus by "providing clear links to syllabus outcomes", "enabling students to demonstrate the levels of achievement outlined in the Performance Band Descriptions", "applying marking guidelines based on criteria that relate to the quality of the response" and "aligning performance in the examination each year to the standards established for the course."
- "Examination questions may require students to integrate knowledge, understanding and skills developed through studying the course. The Year 11 course is assumed knowledge for the Year 12 course. An understanding of the Python programming language is also assumed."
- "Some students with disability may be eligible for HSC exam provisions" (https://www.nsw.gov.au/education-and-training/nesa/hsc/exam-provisions).

### 4.3 What the 2025 HSC exam shows about question types

This is an observation of one exam (the only published one), not a NESA specification of future papers. Source: [MG-2025] including its mapping grid, and the marker feedback on [EXAM-2025].

- **27 numbered questions, 80 marks in total** (I summed the marks in the mapping grid: 80). Several questions have parts: 15 (a)-(b), 16 (a)-(c), 23 (a)-(c).
- **Questions 1–14: single items worth 1 or 2 marks** (Q1–11 one mark each; Q12 and Q13 two marks; Q14 one mark). Formats visible in the guidelines: identify the correct answer, a correct item from each dropdown (Q2, Q14), match characteristics to approaches (Q9: ORM versus SQL), "identifies ALL the items" checkbox items (Q10, Q11), six true/false checkboxes (Q12, 2 marks), a table of timing and source-code access for SAST/DAST strategies (Q13).
- **Q15 (a)** (3 marks): "Correctly completes ALL the dropdowns" (data dictionary). **Q23 (c)** (3 marks): "Provides the correct response for ALL items" (fundamental software design security concepts).
- NESA's marker feedback groups the questions under the heading **"Short-answer items"** starting at **Q15 (b)** and continuing to Q27. The guidelines and feedback do **not** label each item by type, so classifying Q15 (a) and Q23 (c) as objective-response is my inference from their dropdown/select format. On that reading the paper has about 16 objective-response items (Q1–14, 15 (a), 23 (c); 22 marks) and 16 short-answer items (Q15 (b) to Q27; 58 marks), which sits inside NESA's stated ranges (14–18 and 16–18 items; approx. 20 and 60 marks). Treat this split as inferred, not published.
- **Short-answer items and marks** ([MG-2025]): 3 marks for most (Q15 (b), 16 (a)-(c), 17, 18, 19, 20, 22, 23 (a)-(b)); **Q21 4 marks** (SQL query); **Q24 5 marks**, **Q25 5 marks**, **Q26 5 marks** (Python program); **Q27 6 marks** (video stimulus). Five items are worth 4 or more marks, consistent with "at least three items will be worth 4 to 8 marks". The highest-mark item on the 2025 paper is 6 marks; the sample exam had an 8-mark item.
- **Code and technical response types** (the exam is taken on a computer, with the code editor, drawing tool and SQL/coding environment):
  - Pseudocode algorithm (Q17, 3 marks: sum an array, then IF/CASE classification into High/Medium/Low, using the Course Specifications pseudocode keywords such as REPEAT ... UNTIL, CASEWHERE, ELSEIF).
  - Python program (Q19, 3 marks: string slicing and selection; Q26, 5 marks: generating a list of unique random numbers with the `random` library, selection and looping, formatted grid output). Marker feedback: "develop code that was able to be executed in the coding environment"; areas to improve include "using correct indentation of code" and "calling functions in the main program after they are defined".
  - SQL (Q21, 4 marks): SELECT, WHERE with OR, COUNT, GROUP BY, ORDER BY ... DESC. Feedback: "construct a SQL query that ran in the environment".
  - Class diagram (Q22, 3 marks): inheritance and removing redundant attributes. Feedback: "using appropriate symbols for class diagrams".
- **Extended and applied written responses with stimulus:** Q16 (weather app: PWA versus interactive website, load times, privacy by design), Q18 (metadata and data mining for a streaming service), Q20 (session management and code efficiency), Q23 (a)-(c) (food-delivery website: social and ethical issues, server-side programming), Q24 (AI safety monitoring system and workers), Q25 (neural network and a virtual personal assistant, with one positive and one negative impact), Q27 (human bias and the usefulness of AI, "Support your answer with reference to the video"). Command verbs used in the marking criteria: identifies, outlines, describes, explains, compares, discusses, constructs, provides. Feedback repeatedly asks students to "engage with any stimulus material provided" and use "course specific terms".
- **Content coverage** (from the mapping grid, which cites "Content" and syllabus outcomes for each question): all 27 questions are mapped to Year 12 focus areas and to Year 12 outcomes (SE-12-01 to SE-12-09): Software engineering project (implementation methods, development approaches, data structures, data dictionary, testing and debugging, algorithm design, modelling tools, language-dependent optimisation, design/construct/implement), Programming for the web, Secure software architecture, Software automation (ML training, DevOps, logistic regression, safety of workers, patterns in human behaviour, bias in AI). No question is mapped to a Year 11 outcome, although NESA states the Year 11 course is assumed knowledge.
- **Discrepancy noticed:** the mapping grid lists Q16 (a) as "load times" and Q16 (b) as "interactive websites and PWAs", but the marking criteria and the sample full-mark PDF show 16 (a) as the PWA comparison and 16 (b) as load times. The grid labels appear swapped; the criteria are taken as correct here.
- **Sample full-mark responses PDF** covers Q16, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25 and 27 (not Q15 (b), 17 or 26). It states that "the word count provided for each short-answer item is a guide only".

## 5. Course standards [STANDARDS]

"Course standards are comprised of syllabus standards and performance standards. Syllabus outcomes and content form the syllabus standards that describe what students are expected to know, understand and do. Performance standards, including the Common Grade Scale for Preliminary courses, HSC Performance Band Descriptions and Achievement Level Descriptions (for HSC courses with grades only) describe how well students have achieved in relation to syllabus standards. Performance standards are used to report student achievement on NESA credentials."

- Year 11: "The Common Grade Scale for Preliminary courses provides generic, holistic descriptions for performance at each of the 5 grade levels. It is used to report student achievement in Year 11 courses in all NSW schools." The A–E descriptors are a shared NESA document; they are not transcribed here.
- HSC: "HSC performance band descriptions summarise the knowledge, understanding and skills typically demonstrated by students at each band level in a particular course." Each band is introduced "A student who performs at this level typically:". The Software Engineering descriptors, verbatim:

**Band 6:** demonstrates comprehensive understanding of the influence of technologies on the development of software engineering solutions; demonstrates extensive knowledge and understanding of the social, ethical and legal implications of the application of software engineering solutions on the individual, society and the environment; applies sophisticated technical skills in the design and implementation of programming solutions; collects, uses, manages and stores complex data effectively, safely and securely; applies comprehensive skills in developing, managing and documenting software engineering projects; communicates logically and effectively using a range of terms, conventions and methods.

**Band 5:** demonstrates broad understanding of the influence of technologies on the development of software engineering solutions; demonstrates thorough knowledge and understanding of the social, ethical and legal implications of the application of software engineering solutions on the individual, society and the environment; applies high-level technical skills in the design and implementation of programming solutions; collects, uses, manages and stores data effectively, safely and securely; demonstrates high-level skills in developing, managing and documenting software engineering projects; communicates logically, using appropriate terms, conventions and methods.

**Band 4:** demonstrates sound understanding of the influence of technologies on the development of software engineering solutions; demonstrates sound knowledge and understanding of the social, ethical and legal implications of the application of software engineering solutions; applies sound technical skills in the design and implementation of programming solutions; collects, uses, manages and stores data safely and securely; demonstrates sound skills in developing, managing and documenting software engineering projects; communicates using relevant terms, conventions and methods.

**Band 3:** demonstrates basic understanding of the influence of technologies on the development of software engineering solutions; demonstrates basic knowledge and understanding of the social and/or ethical and/or legal implications of the application of software engineering solutions; displays basic skills in programming; uses data safely and securely; displays basic skills in developing software engineering projects; communicates using basic terms and conventions and/or methods.

**Band 2:** demonstrates limited understanding of the use of technologies; displays limited skills in programming; displays limited skills in developing software engineering projects; communicates with limited use of terms.

**Band 1:** "Performance reported as Band 1 indicates that the minimum standard expected was not demonstrated."
